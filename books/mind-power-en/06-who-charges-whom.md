# Chapter Six: Who Charges Whom

All the tools we have talked about so far were in one place: inside your head. The Watcher on the platform, the bill, the inner words, the images, the one minute... all inner work.

This chapter steps outside a little. Because there is a truth every success book leaves wide open: you are not a brain floating in the air. You are a body... and the body gets charged and drained. And what this chapter talks about is the most practical lesson in the book after the one-minute rule.

## The Story of Tarek and the Phone Charger

Tarek is a designer at an advertising company. And Tarek's phone? In the best condition possible: a charger in the car, a charger in the bag, a power bank always fully charged. The battery never drops below half... and if it does, Tarek gets genuinely tense and hunts for the nearest socket.

Tarek himself? Look at him on a Monday night... this is it: four and a half hours of sleep (the apps chapter's bill — remember it?), a quick cup of coffee on the run, nine hours sitting at a desk, and the closest thing to movement in his day is running after the microbus... and at night, the gathering.

The gathering: the same friends, for years. The same chairs, the same coffee, and the same topics word for word: the country, the prices, the manager, the money, and what happened and never came... three hours of continuous complaining, nine to midnight. Tarek loves them with a true love. But he suffers from something vague: he gets up from the gathering completely drained. He blames it on "work tiredness"... but he is drained without knowing why.

The scene that opened his eyes was small: his phone died in the middle of an important call about a side project he had dreamed of for years... and he sat after the call asking himself, for the first time, a question both silly and terrifying:

"What percentage am I at?"

He does everything for his phone: charges it, adjusts its brightness, closes the apps eating its battery, updates it every month. And his own self — the device that dreams, plans, loves, and walks the earth — has never been treated as a device that needs charging at all.

He ran a one-week experiment he called, for himself, "the charging week": fixed his sleep at one bedtime, walked twenty minutes every day, cut one day only from the gathering... and put in its place one thing he knows lifts his mood (reading before sleep).

The result after a week? Not a miracle — there are no miracles in this book. But Tarek found himself coming home from work with life in him, and preparing for the side project at night... for the first time in three years. Same job, same salary, same commute... one device got charged right, so it started working right.

And the most important part of that week: Tarek cut off none of his friends — he only adjusted the distribution. And he found among them one who was different: an old friend who watches documentaries and talks about things he is actually doing... this one charges him. Tarek tuned his network... and the network started charging him back.

## The Idea in Points

### First: Your willpower is a battery... not a factory

No one has infinite willpower. And decisions consume it: what to wear, what to eat for breakfast, answer now or later, start or wait a little... every small decision takes a piece of your battery. That is why at the end of the day you find yourself eating something you are not hungry for, and watching something you are not enjoying — this is not weakness... this is a battery running out.

Routine is the engineering solution to this problem: when you fix the time of your sleep, your waking, your food, and your work... you lift hundreds of decisions off your back every day. A frozen decision takes no energy.

That is why the person with a routine is not stronger than you — he simply pays less. And the energy difference he saves is what makes some people open a project after a full workday... while others say "no energy" while resting at home.

### Second: You are a charging network inside a charging network

Something we all forget: the body that carries your brain needs conditions to work. Sleep is not a luxury — sleep is the charging of the device itself. Food is fuel. Movement is activation. Daylight sets your internal clock. And the people around you... are either chargers or drainers.

We audit everything in our lives: an expenses audit, an internet audit, a fuel audit — and we never audit our energy even once a year. Even though money, if it goes, comes back... and energy drained in a single ordinary day, gone for good. The energy review is the most precious review of your life... and it takes ten minutes.

### Third: There is what charges you... and what drains you

This is not an invitation to cut people off or live in an ivory tower — far from it. But the truth must be said as it is: there are gatherings you leave feeling lighter... and gatherings you leave feeling heavier, having learned nothing new and moved nothing at all.

And the test could not be simpler: after any gathering or call... ask yourself one question: do I feel lighter or heavier? If lighter — that is a charger. If heavier — that is a drainer. And if the drainer is your friends, people you love? Do not cut them off... adjust the dose, and reduce the frequency. The goal is not to isolate yourself from the world... the goal is that your time and energy get distributed with awareness, not by habit.

### Fourth: The environment takes decisions instead of you... before you know it

There is a question smarter than "why don't I do what I care about?": "What is there around me that is stopping me?"

The environment takes decisions on your behalf. The phone next to the bed? Two hours of your sleep were decided... without you noticing. The book on the far shelf? Tonight's reading decision was taken. The app on the first screen? An hour of your attention was taken — without anyone asking you.

The engineering solution: instead of fighting the decision every day, change the place where it gets made. The phone outside the room = the sleep decision made once... instead of battling yourself over it every night. The book on the pillow = the reading decision made. The gym bag in front of the door instead of the far closet = the decision made. A supportive environment does not do the effort for you... but it lifts half your daily battles off your back.

The rule: "I am strong and can resist" is an exhausting plan — while "I am smart and will not put myself in the test in the first place" is a plan that survives. Willpower is for surprises... and environment is for ordinary days. And the people who change their lives are not stronger than you in willpower... they simply let the environment work with them instead of dragging them backwards.

**Environment check — ten quick questions:**
1. Does the phone sleep next to your bed?
2. Is the first thing you open in the morning a screen?
3. Is there a fixed focus spot at home... or is every spot the bed?
4. Is the easiest food available the only food available?
5. Do the tools of the thing you long for require a full "excavation"?
6. Is the gathering that drains you... easier to arrange than any alternative?
7. Is the screen that keeps you up late in the same room as your sleep?
8. Are your workout clothes ready from the night before?
9. Is there a human charger around you... that you are not investing in?
10. Is there a drainer taking from you by habit, not by choice?

Every "yes" is not an accusation — it is a ready adjustment point. And every point you fix... turns a hundred daily battles into one decision, made once and done.

## Chapter Exercise: The Charger Audit

One week, a paper and pen, and three decisions only — three, no more:

1. **Write two lists.** "Chargers": five things you know from your own experience charge you (enough sleep? walking? reading? prayer? a call with a particular person?). And "drainers": five things that empty you (the morning phone? a specific gathering? comparisons? late nights on YouTube?).

2. **Choose only three decisions for the week.** One about sleep (the same bedtime every day), one about movement (a twenty-minute walk), and one about people or the screen (remove one thing from the drainers list and reduce it... and put one of the chargers in its place).

3. **Every night: what is your energy out of ten?** One number only on the paper. No analysis, no diary writing — a number.

4. **End of the week: compare the first day to the last.** And choose one decision only to become permanent, without thinking — this is the seed of your new routine.

5. **Forbidden to add anything new.** This week is not a training load... this week is optimal operation of the battery you have right now.

**And if the week breaks in the middle** — travel, illness, circumstances, or just life: same smallest-step rule. "I'll restart the whole week" is a mountain plan — take one day instead: the three decisions today, only. The week did not close... it just got shorter. And the routine that can survive a crack is the one that lives.

**And if you are unsure where to begin:** fix sleep first. The sleep decision is the mother of decisions — it is the main charger for everything else, and the first thing to fall when the environment gets tangled. Fix your sleep... and you have won half the battle before it begins.

**The sign of success:** there will be at least one day where you do something without "deciding" it — put on your shoes and walk without an inner negotiation, or grab the book before feeling like it. That moment means the routine charged you instead of you paying from your willpower... the device started charging itself.

## Sentence of the Week

"You are the battery... the phone is just a device."

This week, take care of your battery the way you take care of your phone: see when you get charged and when you get drained, and close the apps eating it in the background. Because the device you carry with you every day and love... is you.
