# Chapter Four: I Found the Opportunity

## The story

On a quiet residential street, there was a small closed shop.

Four years. The shutter down, the sign faded, and the people of the street so used to the look of a closed place that they no longer looked at it.

A man named Tarek came, stood in front of it, and asked.

Who owns the shop? An old man, seventy-four, who used to run a carpentry workshop. He had shut it when his son travelled, he had no one to help, and he kept saying: "There's no work like the old days."

Tarek sat with him for a month. Not to buy. To understand.

And he heard things:
- Everyone on the street said, "If there were someone here who fixed wooden doors, we'd be relieved."
- Every time someone brought a carpenter from far away, it took two days of waiting and the cost of travel.
- The old man himself could still fix things — he just couldn't stand for six hours.

So Tarek did something strange: he agreed with the man to open the shop only two days a week, brought him a lad to help with lifting and delivery, and took a cut for organising and delivering.

The shop had been closed four years. Dozens of people saw it every day, and nobody saw the opportunity in it. Not because they were stupid. Because they were looking at **the closed shop**, not at **the repeated complaint**.

And notice one detail in this story, because it matters more than all the numbers: Tarek didn't bring a new idea. He invented nothing. He heard a complaint that existed, saw a resource that existed (a man who could still fix things), and saw the gap between them (nobody was bringing the work to the house). The opportunity was in **the arrangement**, not in the invention.

## An opportunity isn't an idea... an opportunity is demand

This is the most important sentence in the chapter:

**An opportunity isn't a nice idea in your head. An opportunity is a problem that exists, and someone willing to pay for it to be solved.**

And the difference matters a lot, because most people look for opportunity in the wrong place: inside themselves. "What do I want to do?" instead of "What are people annoyed by every day?"

The one who looks inside finds hobbies. The one who looks outside finds a market.

And there's another reason people look inside: looking inside is comfortable. Nobody will say "no" to you, nobody will cut you off. Looking outside requires asking, hearing things you don't like, and discovering that your idea of many years... nobody wants it.

## Three sources where opportunities are born

### Source one: the repeated complaint

Listen for the complaint that repeats more than once in the same week, from the same kind of person.

Not any complaint. The complaint people say to each other and then apologise for: "nobody understands," "they're all crooks," "the service is slow," "nobody pays attention."

A repeated complaint is **demand written in the language of anger.**

And here's a simple drill that opens your ears: in any gathering, when someone complains, ask one question: "And what did you do?" The answer tells you everything. If he says "I did everything and there's no solution," that's real pain and existing demand. If he says "nothing," then the complaint is a talking habit, not demand.

### Source two: the missing thing

Keep one question as a habit: "What's missing here?"

In any place, any job, any group online. The missing thing is the space nobody occupies. And people get used to what's missing until it's like air.

Small examples from daily life:
- Nobody answers the phone after six in this particular trade.
- Nobody explains in Arabic in a certain field; everything is in English.
- Nobody delivers to the far areas on the same day.
- Nobody takes small jobs (an hour's work), and people need that hour.

None of these is a genius idea. Each is **a gap**. And a gap gets filled by someone — and if you're the first, you're the one who takes it.

### Source three: the abandoned skill

There are skills in the people around you, and in yourself, left alone because they're "not real work." The man who "fixes anything in the house," the woman who "can talk to anyone and make them understand," the one who "can tell a story and make people listen."

An abandoned skill is an opportunity because you start from something you're stronger than average at, and you don't need two years of learning.

And one question uncovers these quickly: **"What do people ask me to help them with, that I enjoy so much I don't feel it's work?"**

That answer is usually not on your CV, and it may have been under your feet for years.

## Opportunity in a crisis

Every crisis changes demand. And when demand changes fast, the gaps where the big players fail open up for the small ones.

Before a crisis, people buy from one known place. After a crisis, there are people looking for an alternative, willing to try, and with no objection to trying someone they've never heard of.

And in exactly that period: **opportunity equals chaos.**

And the honest thing to say: a crisis is not a blessing. We won't say "every crisis is an opportunity" — that's empty motivational talk. A crisis is real pain. But pain opens doors that were closed, and the one who looks with an employee's eye finds a problem, while the one who looks with a searcher's eye finds demand.

And there's an important condition for a crisis to be an opportunity for you: **you have to be close to the pain.** The one working in the market finds it; the one reading about it online finds news. And that closeness isn't luck — it's the choice to be present.

## The eye of the searcher

What's the difference between the two?

**The one waiting:** "There's nothing here." And he sits down.
**The one searching:** "There's nothing here — so what's **missing**, and why hasn't anyone done it?"

One question opens the world: **why?**

- Why is nobody selling this nearby? (Maybe it's hard — or maybe nobody thought of it.)
- Why do people keep looking for someone from outside? (There's unmet demand.)
- Why did this idea fail before? (Did it fail in the wrong place, at the wrong time, or in the execution?)

And the question that shortens the road: **what repeats?** The repeated complaint, the repeated request, the repeated question. Repetition is the market's voice.

And a third question that's very useful and saves years: **"Who is paying right now, and paying whom?"**

If nobody is paying, then you have interest, not a market. Interest gives you admiration; payment gives you an opportunity. And admiration is the cheapest thing on the internet.

## So what isn't an opportunity?

For the opportunity to show, you need to know the shape of what isn't one:

**1) The idea only you find beautiful.** Everyone said "nice idea" and nobody paid. That's not an opportunity, that's polite admiration.

**2) The thing people need but never paid for.** They need it for free. That's lovely, but it doesn't feed anyone.

**3) A market where giants do the same thing at half the price.** You can't compete unless you have something you're closer at: smaller size, more speed, finer service, or a specific audience they don't care about.

**4) An idea that needs years of learning before the first pound.** Not impossible, but not a first step. Call it a goal, and put it in the second column of your life.

## The opportunity sheet

An opportunity doesn't come down from the sky. It gets written on paper. Three columns:

| Demand | Problem | What I have |
|---|---|---|
| Who asks? Asks for what? | What's the real pain? | Skill / time / equipment / knowledge |

And when you fill the sheet in, look for the **intersection**.

Demand with no problem = no opportunity in it.
A problem with no demand = charity, not a project.
Demand + pain + something you can start = an opportunity.

And add a fourth column if you can — the one that determines the money: **"Who pays? And do they pay from their own pocket or from a company's pocket?"**

Personal payment is faster and closes faster. Company payment is slower but grows with time.

## Signs of a real opportunity

**1) People are already trying to solve it themselves.** If people are going around in circles and paying more to reach a bad solution, there's demand.

**2) It repeats.** Once might be a coincidence. Ten times is a market.

**3) It doesn't require a year of education.** If it needs a new qualification, it might be a big opportunity, but it'll be a life project, not a step.

**4) Someone could pay for it, even a little.** Any amount. A small amount proves demand; free talk proves nothing.

**5) You can build a miniature version this week.** If not, then you're talking about a dream, not an opportunity.

And a sixth sign, the one that separates those who start from those who watch: **you find yourself thinking about it when you don't have to.** Not during "opportunity hunting time" — in the middle of work, on the road. The idea that comes back on its own has internal speed, and internal speed is what carries you after the enthusiasm fades.

## And the opportunity doesn't arrive drawn

Opportunities arrive in uncomfortable clothes. In the shape of someone's complaint, or something missing, or someone in front of you trying to solve something himself and failing. It rarely arrives as "an idea that came to me."

That's why most people walk past it every day. Because it doesn't carry a sign. It only carries repetition.

## Exercise: the opportunity sheet

For three days, record:

1. **Every complaint** you heard from someone (word for word).
2. **A question mark** every time you catch yourself saying "there's nothing here" or "nobody does this."
3. **A skill** that someone around you — or you — has, which they call "a simple thing."

Before you sleep, look for repetition. The complaint you wrote twice, the skill that appeared in two situations — those are the first thread.

And after three days, write one line at the bottom of the page:

**"The complaint I heard more than once is ______, the person who could solve it is ______, and I could start on it by ______."**

If you can complete that line without lying to yourself, then you've found your first real opportunity and you're looking it in the face, not waiting for it.

## The difference between "an idea" and "an opportunity"

People confuse the two, and the confusion costs years.

**An idea** is something in your head. Nice, possibly brilliant, with no demand for it yet.
**An opportunity** is an idea **somebody asked for**. There is a person, now, ready to pay or move for it.

And the difference isn't quality. It's **the existence of a person.**

That's why one question separates them: "Who asked me for this?" If there's no name, it's an idea, not an opportunity. And if there's a name, you've already started — even if you haven't done anything yet.

And the good thing is that ideas don't get lost. An idea nobody wants today might be an opportunity in two years when the market changes. An idea waits; an opportunity moves.

## Why do people miss obvious opportunities?

Three reasons, all rational from the inside:

**First: the opportunity arrives ordinary.** It doesn't arrive as a "project"; it arrives as someone saying "do you know how to do this?" And people wait for the big shape, so they walk past the opportunity every day.

**Second: the opportunity arrives tiring.** Any real opportunity adds work, at a time when you're already not free. And people feel a real opportunity should be "an open door," not "extra work."

**Third: the opportunity is frightening.** Because if it succeeds, you become responsible. And that responsibility is what makes people prefer "there are no opportunities" to "an opportunity that needs work."

These three aren't an information problem. They're a readiness problem. That's why this book doesn't offer you an opportunity — it offers you **a way to see what you already have**.

## Something small before you leave this chapter

Take a sheet of paper and write three names: people you spoke with in the last two weeks who complained about something.

Not a big complaint. An ordinary one: "I can't find anyone to sort out...", "I don't know how to...", "every time I'm looking for...".

Then next to each name, write one line: **"Do I know how to do this?"**

The one you answer "yes" to isn't an exercise. That's a first client.

## The opportunities that walked past you

A small exercise that reveals your eye more than any words: take a sheet and write **three opportunities that walked past you in the last two years.**

Not the big ones from films. The ordinary ones: a job someone offered you and you said "not now," a client you could have brought in, a free course you didn't sign up for, someone who asked for your help and you apologised.

Then next to each one, write: **what sentence did you say to yourself at the time?**

You'll find repeated answers: "not the time," "I won't manage," "it won't work with me," "he won't agree." And those sentences aren't analysis — they're bodyguards.

The important thing here: **an opportunity isn't lost because it's hard; it's lost because rejection arrives faster than thinking.** The mind rejects in a second, and then goes looking for a reason to justify the rejection.

And the cure is simple and annoying: the **"24 hours before refusing"** rule. Any new opportunity — you're not allowed to refuse it the same day. Take it, write it down, come back to it tomorrow. Most quick refusals turn into "let me ask" when you sleep on them.

## Something small before you leave this chapter

A question keeps coming up: **"I don't even know what my resources are."**

The solution isn't to think more. The solution is to ask three people: one from your work, one from your family, and one who works with you on something else. Ask each one question: **"What do you see me doing better than other people?"**

The answers will surprise you, because people see in you the thing you consider "ordinary." And what's ordinary in you is a resource in someone else's hands.

## The last question of the chapter

If you knew there was demand that exists, that nobody serves well, and that you could serve — what's the one thing that could stop you?

If the answer was "I don't know where to start," then you don't lack an opportunity, you lack a small first step. And that step doesn't need money or a plan; it needs you to ask one person: "If I brought you this, what would you take it for?"

## The sentence of the week

**Nobody makes an opportunity out of nothing... people buy a solution to a pain that repeated.**
