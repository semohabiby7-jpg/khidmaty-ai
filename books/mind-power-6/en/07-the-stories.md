# Chapter Seven: Stories

These stories aren't extraordinary success stories. There are no millionaires in them, no transformations overnight. There are ordinary people, in narrow boxes, who came out of them the same way this whole book talks about.

Read them one at a time. And after each story, ask yourself one question: **Which one am I?**

---

## 1) Mahmoud — the one who was waiting for permission

Mahmoud is a mechanic in a workshop. Very good. And his friends have been telling him for years: "Open your own workshop. You're better than the man you work for."

And Mahmoud sits and explains: "A workshop needs capital, it needs a licence, it needs premises, and electricity is expensive."

And he's been working on the idea of the workshop in his head for eight years.

The truth is that Mahmoud was good at something other than mechanics: he was good at finding reasons. And whenever anyone came near the idea of the workshop, he had a reason ready. And the reason wasn't a lie — the capital really wasn't there, and the licence really does need procedures. But those reasons were working very well at one thing: **relieving him of trying.**

Until one day, a man came to him to have his car repaired, and said: "I can't find anyone to come to the house and do maintenance. I'm not free to go to the workshop."

Mahmoud said: "I can come."

And Mahmoud didn't mean to start a business. He meant to finish the man's errand.

In the first month, he made five house visits, one of them preventive maintenance. And with time, he was able to establish a few things:

- **His tools were all there.** He didn't need capital, he needed to use what he had.
- **Premises weren't necessary.** The customer has premises — his house.
- **The commercial licence** could wait until there was steady income.

And after four months, two things happened he hadn't expected. The first: he started charging more than the workshop, because the customer pays for not having to come down to you. And the second: he had free time in the workshop, because he was working by appointment, not by standing around.

The workshop he'd been waiting eight years for was, in reality, **a car, a tool bag, and a phone number.**

And the lesson: Mahmoud wasn't waiting for permission from anyone. He was waiting for **a complete moment**. And a complete moment doesn't come. The opportunity arrives incomplete, and the completion happens while you're working.

And there's another lesson hidden in the story: his first customer came to him **while he wasn't looking for one**. Mahmoud wasn't marketing, he had no page, he ran no adverts. All he did was say "I can come" instead of "that won't work."

And that sentence — "I can come" — is the cheapest sentence in the world, and the most expensive one at the same time.

## 2) Nagat — the home that was her box

Nagat has a home, a husband who works abroad, and two children.

And she used to say: "I'm imprisoned in the house."

And she told that story every time, with real sadness, and nobody could say anything to her, because what she said was true: the house really does take her time.

But there was a small detail nobody noticed, not even her: Nagat spent an hour every night on her phone, reading and following educational videos, and writing notes in a notebook. For a whole year.

Meaning the house was her box, and at the same time it was the place where she was learning.

Until one day her children were in a lesson, and she was sitting beside them, and she asked the teacher a question about a particular lesson. The teacher paused, and said: "Could you repeat the question?"

Nagat repeated the question. The teacher asked: "What do you do for a living?"

She said: "I'm at home."

He said: "No — your question is the question of someone who understands the material."

Nagat took the course. Some time later, the course needed an assistant. Nagat started teaching online, from home.

An important note here: it took two years before this became steady income. There was no leap. And there were many months when she thought about quitting.

And the hardest month was the seventh. Ill, the children in exams, and the income still small. She sat and told her husband on the phone: "I'm quitting, this isn't the time." And her husband said a simple sentence: "Fine, quit for one month only, and then decide."

The eighth month was better. Not because the world had changed, but because she was in a different month. And the difference between the two was one month.

And what made the difference for her wasn't that she became a doctor. What made the difference was that she started using **a resource she had been seeing as a complaint**: the house, and the time inside it.

And a third thing, the most important: Nagat wasn't "sacrificing." She was doing something in time that already existed. The phone hour she used to spend scrolling became an hour of learning. The same hour, a different use.

## 3) Uncle Saber — the seventy-year-old who opened

Saber was a carpenter, and he closed his business at seventy.

And when his family said to him, "That's it, rest," he did rest. Two months. And after that he had a strange anger whose source he couldn't identify, and he kept discovering that he was fixing things in the house that didn't need fixing.

And he would wake early and go down and stand by the workshop he'd left. Stand at the door, look, and go back.

And once, his grandson — his daughter's son, a boy in secondary school — was looking for a school project. Saber fixed a broken chair for him, and called him over and said: "Look, I don't want to work with you. I want to teach you to do this with your own hands."

And he taught him. And the boy made his project, and the work became known in the family.

And once, the boy said to him: "Some people are saying they want a broken chair fixed. Could we do that?"

And Saber did it, but on one condition: **he works three hours a week, and he doesn't hold all the work in his own hands.**

And this is the most important thing in his story: the only thing Uncle Saber let go of in his life was letting go of his own hands.

And another thing worth saying: Uncle Saber stopped "working." He started "teaching." And the difference in feeling is big. The one who works feels he's running. The one who teaches feels that what he did over a whole lifetime now has an extension.

And the lesson: the box isn't always narrow from the outside. Saber's box had a key in it, and it was locked from the inside with one sentence: **"I've done my part."**

And that sentence isn't a lie. It's true from the point of view of work, and harmful from the point of view of life.

## 4) Karim — the young man who "had nothing in him"

Karim graduated, and sat at home for a year and a half.

And his family said of him: "He doesn't try," and his friends said: "Difficult."

And Karim thought exactly the same. And he was working on one thing, and there was someone asking him about it: **computer things**. He removed viruses, fitted RAM, and taught things he called "simple."

And he used to say: "Anyone knows this." Because he didn't count it as a skill. He counted it as a habit.

But there was something he did that he wasn't noticing: he sat with everyone for two hours. Not for the work — to explain. And people felt they had understood something for the first time.

And once, one of his friends said to him: "Take two hundred pounds and sort out my laptop."

Karim said: "I don't need money."

His friend said: "I need to pay, so that next time I can ask you again."

A small sentence, but it took him out of his box: **money isn't money. Money is proof that the thing has value.**

Karim started taking a small payment. And after six months, he had fifteen regular customers. And after a year, he was opening a small repair shop with his friend.

And in one of the months, one of his neighbours, twenty years older than him, said to him: "This patience you have with people isn't patience — it's work."

And it was the first time Karim saw something he did without effort that was, in reality, the most valuable thing he had.

And his family still say: "Looks like he's doing something now."

And the lesson: Karim wasn't short of a skill. He was short of a **correct valuation** of the skill. And young people often do real work every day, and count it as "a normal thing," and then say there's "nothing in them."

## What the four stories say

**1) The first obstacle isn't money.** All four had clear resources, and all four thought of them as "little."

**2) The opportunity came in the shape of a request from someone** — not in the shape of an idea from their own head. Not one of them sat down to make a plan. All of them responded to something in front of them.

**3) The first step was extremely small.** A car and a bag, a question in a lesson, a broken chair, two hundred pounds.

**4) There was no leap.** There's a lot of time between the beginning and stability. And that time isn't empty — it's the time in which they change.

**5) The sentence that repeats with all of them:** "I didn't know this was possible." And it's the closest sentence to the one we want to hear from you at the end of the book.

## What the stories say about the box itself

In every story, the door was locked from the inside, and all of them were holding the handle:

- Mahmoud was waiting for capital. And the door was "I can come."
- Nagat was waiting for time. And the door was the phone hour.
- Saber was waiting for rest. And the door was "I'll teach you."
- Karim was waiting for value. And the door was "take two hundred pounds."

Not one of them broke his door down by force. All of them opened it. And the opening was so quiet that nobody noticed it, themselves included.

## How to use these stories

Not every story will work for you. But there's a simple method for extracting what concerns you:

1. Choose the story that **made you feel** something — not the one you admired most, the one that hurt most. The pain is the marker.
2. Write one line: "I say to myself the same sentence that ____ said."
3. Write another line: "The opportunity I'm waiting to arrive is, in reality, ____."
4. And write one small thing you could do next week, in the same direction as what they did.

The third line is the one that matters. Because it exposes what you're waiting for.

## 5) Salma — the one who thought she was late

Salma was forty-one, and had worked fifteen years in an administrative job.

And she said one sentence every time anyone suggested she start something: "I've missed the chance. The one who starts in his twenties wins; for me now, it won't work."

And that sentence was comfortable, because it closed the door before anyone knocked.

And once, the company did "reductions," and her name came up. And the surprise was that she wasn't upset the way she expected. She was upset, and then she felt something strange: lightness.

She sat at home for three months, and every month said "next month I'll start." Until her neighbour — an old woman of seventy-four — asked her for a small thing: "Could you sort out these bills for me? I can't see the numbers well."

Salma sorted them out in an hour, put them in one tidy file, and explained everything to her neighbour in simple sentences. The woman said: "This work of yours is better than the accountant I pay."

Salma laughed, and said: "It's a simple thing, I've been doing it for fifteen years."

And the woman said the sentence that broke the box: **"What you've done for fifteen years isn't simple. It's the one thing nobody can compete with you on."**

Salma started with her neighbour, and then two more neighbours. The work wasn't a "project." It was a small service called "sorting bills and files."

After a year, she had eleven regular clients, working from home four days a week, and earning slightly less than her old job — but earning it in half the hours.

And strangest of all, she said the last time: "If they hadn't let me go, I'd still be saying next month."

That doesn't mean being sacked is a good thing. It means the sentence that used to frighten her ("I've missed the chance") was bigger than the truth. Salma wasn't late — she was sitting waiting for something that wasn't coming, and calling the waiting "lateness."

## A fifth thing shared by all five stories

In all five stories, there's something nobody said plainly, but it's present in every one:

**All of them had the work ready long ago.** Mahmoud had been repairing for years. Nagat had been learning for years. Saber had been a carpenter his whole life. Karim had been taking devices apart for years. Salma had been sorting papers for fifteen years.

Meaning the opportunity didn't need a new skill. It needed **a new name** for the skill that already existed.

And that's very good news and very hard news at the same time. Good, because you don't need to start from zero. Hard, because you won't be able to say "I don't know what to do" — you know, you just haven't admitted it.

## Two questions after the stories

If you want to really benefit from this chapter, answer these two questions on paper, in your own hand:

**The first:** "What is the thing I've been doing for years that people say 'thank you' to me about, and that I call 'a simple thing'?"

**The second:** "Who is the first person I could say to tomorrow: I do such and such, and I could do it for you?"

The first reveals the skill. The second turns it into an opportunity. And if you write the first and close the paper, then you've read a nice chapter, and moved on.

## A short fifth story: the one who opened the door for someone else

There are people who came out of their box, and then did something stranger: they went back and opened the door for someone else.

One of them worked in a school. Years of saying "I'm not an admin person," until they needed someone to sort out the exam timetable, and nobody could. So she sorted it in one night. And afterwards she sorted it again. And after a year, she was the one training other teachers on that timetable.

The story isn't that she "discovered a talent." The story is that she did a small thing they needed, and said a new sentence to herself: **"I know how to organise."**

And after two years, she had a small workshop. And when they asked her, how did you start? she said a sentence worth writing on a wall:

"I didn't do anything new. I just did the thing in front of me, and then I named it."

**Naming** is the second half that gets forgotten. The one who does something and doesn't name it keeps doing it for free and thinks of it as "a simple thing." And the one who names it finds people asking for it by name.

## What repeats in all the stories

If you go back to the five stories, you'll find the same thing repeating:

**None of them started with a big dream.** They started with something in front of them, small, that was needed.

**All of them heard a sentence about themselves**, and carried it for years, until the moment the sentence quarrelled with reality.

**All of them did something smaller than they imagined**, and something bigger than they expected came out of it.

**And none of them came out alone.** Every one of them needed someone: someone who taught him, someone who encouraged her, someone who asked him for the first service.

And the last lesson is the most important: **the opportunity doesn't arrive written with your name on it.** It arrives in the shape of a request from someone, or a recurring pain, or a skill you see as ordinary. And the one who knows how to read it finds it was in front of him for years.

## The sentence of the week

**The opportunity arrives in the shape of someone's need... not in the shape of a big sign.**

## And the last chapter

That's it. We've known the box, taken it apart, tried the outside, found the opportunity, expanded, and opened for others.

There's one question left, and it isn't an easy one: **Will you stay like this?**
