# Chapter One: Your Mind Is Not You

There is one question that, if you learn to answer it correctly, will change your life from its place — with no course and no other book: when you say "I"... who exactly are you talking about?

Most of us answer without thinking: "I means me. This person. The one who moves, speaks, and makes decisions." A logical answer... but incomplete. Because what happens in the next few lines is the cornerstone this whole book is built on. If you understand this chapter well, the six chapters ahead will open for you effortlessly.

And we will begin the way we always do... with a story.

## Hisham and the Ten Lost Minutes

Hisham is an employee who rides the metro every morning. A trip from his home at the edge of the city to his job downtown. He knows this trip by heart: walk down the stairs, pass the gate, stop at the same spot on track two, get off at the fourth station. A trip he has made more than a thousand times.

That day was an ordinary day, like every day. Normal crowds. A car packed to the brim. Hisham found a standing spot by the door, held the rail, and let his mind wander off on its own.

And the mind went. It went to the twelve o'clock meeting waiting for him at work. To the reply he should have given his manager yesterday. To the call with his friend who got upset two weeks ago, and neither of them has called the other since. To a side project he has been thinking about for two months. To the phone bill and the shared subscription. To a word his wife said that morning before he left the house... and he kept chasing it.

The car arrived at the fourth station. People got off. Hisham got off after them, on autopilot, on the route he had taken a thousand times.

And as he walked through the station tunnel, the strange thing happened: he stopped suddenly.

Not because he forgot something. Not because he saw something. He stopped because of one small question that knocked on his head without warning:

"Where was I for the last ten minutes?"

He tried to remember. Nothing. Not one second. Ten full minutes of his life... that did not exist in his memory. His body was standing in the car, his hand on the rail, his eyes open, his ears hearing the announcements. Everything in him was present. At the station, in the car, on the way. Everything... except him.

He stood in the middle of the tunnel and said to himself the sentence that would come back to his mind years later, and every time he remembers it he feels something cold on his back:

"I was present in all of those places... except my own."

Ten minutes is not much, you might say. Fine — let's do some simple math. Ten minutes on each trip, two trips a day = twenty minutes. In a month = ten hours. In a year = more than five full days of life.

Five full days... and he was not in them. He did not travel or move or experience anything... he simply was not there.

But the important part of Hisham's story is not the numbers. The important part is that, standing in the tunnel, he did something very few people do: he noticed that there is a difference between his body, which was riding the metro... and "him," who was somewhere else entirely. That this difference is real. And that this was the first time he had felt it in years.

## The Station Watcher

In that same metro, there is an older man who works as the station watcher. He stands on the platform from morning to sunset, watching the trains come and go. He sees a hundred trains a day... maybe more. Each one coming from somewhere and heading somewhere, carrying a hundred stories inside it.

This watcher knows the schedule of every line. He knows the stations by name. He knows where the crowds are and where it is empty, and he can predict which train will run late before it runs late. He knows this metro inside and out.

But there is one thing this watcher never does: he never boards the trains.

He is not a passenger. He is a watcher. He sees them all... and enters none of them. The train carrying a wedding, the train where a problem is breaking out, the train full of laughter, the train full of crowds and misery — they all pass in front of him, while he stays fixed in his place: seeing, knowing, untouched by any of them.

And when Hisham stopped in the tunnel, he understood — for the first time — that there are two kinds of people on the platform of the mind:

There are those like the watcher: they watch the trains of their thoughts pass, they know their schedules, and they choose at their own pleasure whether to look at one or not.

And there are those like the mistaken passenger: they stand on the platform, a train suddenly stops in front of them, they find themselves inside it without making any decision, and it carries them to the end of the line... even if that line is heading somewhere they never intended to go.

One of them sees. The other becomes part of what is happening around him.

And your mind — my friend — is exactly this station. And thoughts are the trains.

## The Idea in Points

### First: Your mind is a thought factory that runs without your permission

Nobody asked you: "Would you like to think about what happened at work yesterday? Want me to show you a scary movie about the future? Want me to remind you of something old from 2009?" No. There is no asking permission. This factory runs twenty-four hours a day. It starts working the moment you wake up, before you open your eyes, keeps working while you eat, work, and drive, and sometimes it works while you sleep — in the form of dreams that finish whatever crossed its mind.

Try something right now as you read: take a few calm breaths, and observe. You will find thoughts coming up for you even in this very moment: "This exercise will make me laugh," "I am thirsty," "Should I finish this chapter or come back to it tomorrow?"... The factory does not shut down just because you are reading.

And this is not a flaw in you. This is the operating system. The mind is a tool whose job is producing thoughts, just as the heart is a tool whose job is pumping blood. You do not get angry at your heart for working. The real problem is not that the factory runs... The problem is that none of us knows there is a factory at all, so we assume every train passing on this platform is us.

### Second: You are not the thoughts... you are the one who sees them

This is the most important sentence in the whole book. Take your time with it.

If you can say "I have a thought that I am a failure" — then there is something in you other than the thought. The one who sees cannot be the screen... just as your eye watching the movie cannot be the screen itself. The one watching is one thing... and what is happening is another.

The thought that says "I don't know how" is just a car on the track: it passed, it exists, you can board it... or you can let it pass. The one that tells you "you are a failure" is not you — it is a thought that passed through the platform of your mind. It came from an old experience, or a word you heard years ago, or a comparison that has no business existing. It is information displaying itself on a screen. You are the one watching the screen.

And when you take this distance... things change. Sadness, anxiety, anger, the feeling of failure — all of them become "weather" that passes, not "you." Like the sky and the clouds: the weather clouds over, and clouds move across the sky, but the sky never becomes the clouds. When you say "I am depressed" while wearing the thought... there is no one left to see. When you say "I notice there is depressed weather passing" — there is you. The Watcher. And the Watcher is present in all weather.

### Third: The distance between you and the thought is your freedom

"Whoever does not see does not choose."

If a thought pulls up to the platform and you do not see it — you will not feel it until you are inside it, riding with it to the end of the line, paying its price in time, misery, relationships, and sleepless nights. Exactly like Hisham: ten minutes... without a single second of choice.

But the moment you see the thought approaching, a small distance opens between you. Something like one breath. And in that breath... there is choice.

The thought says "things are hard" — without distance: this misery is you, it arrives without asking. With distance: "I notice a thought saying things are hard... okay, what else?" And from there, you have room to move. The thought says "I am not the kind of person who masters things" — without distance: you shut down. With distance: "That is a thought... it passed. Not an order."

This distance is the difference between being crushed... and being alive. It is the difference between "thoughts control me" and "thoughts pass in front of me." And it — and this is the crucial point — is the first place where "you" are truly free. Because real freedom is not that there are no trains on the tracks... real freedom is that you are the one who chooses whether to board or not.

### Fourth: Watching is not rejection... and the platform is not a prison

There is a common misunderstanding about this training we must close right now: that the goal is to block the thoughts. No — and trying to block them is more dangerous than the thought itself.

Try it yourself: do not think of a white mouse for thirty seconds. Most people end up carrying the mouse on their head the whole time. Why? Your mind does not process negation... it processes the image. The one who tells himself "don't worry" all night is, in practice, memorizing worry with a full memory. Blocking is not watching... blocking is boarding in reverse.

The true watcher does not prevent a train from entering the station. He sees it... and that is exactly why he does not go down with it. The station was built for trains to pass through — the beautiful and the ugly — and no station in the world is judged by the number of trains that entered it. The station is judged by one thing only: is there a watcher standing... or not?

And the beautiful thought is also a train: a thought of hope, a thought of a project, a thought of a solution. The watcher sees these too — and chooses among them. Because the difference between "living with them" and "seeing them" is the difference between the one who moves through his life... and the one life spins around.

See the watching in everyday situations:
- A driver cut you off in traffic? An anger train pulls into the platform at terrifying speed — and you are the one who decides to run after it or let it pass.
- A word was said at a gathering and you are still thinking about it two days later? The thought "he meant something by it" keeps circling around itself — see it, note it if you like... and let it continue its trip.
- A good idea for a problem at work came to you on the commute? Let the watcher catch it before it passes: "I noticed a thought saying: there might be a solution..." — and write it down.

Watching is not rejecting the thought... watching is respecting your right not to be forced to board it.

## Chapter Exercise: Watcher Training

This exercise is simple in form, not easy in practice. But if you do it for one week, you will find that it is the first time in many long years that you look at your mind from the outside.

**What you need:** paper and pen, and five minutes twice a day, for one full week.

1. **Sit in a quiet place.** It does not have to be perfectly quiet — just a place where no one is talking to you. Leave the phone next to you without opening it.

2. **Let your mind bring out what is inside without interfering.** The moment you sit down, the factory will run as usual. Fine. That is exactly what is required.

3. **Every thought that comes up, write it in a fixed format:** "I noticed a thought saying: ..."
   - "I noticed a thought saying: what happened in yesterday's meeting is still on my mind."
   - "I noticed a thought saying: I will not be able to finish what is on me tomorrow."
   - "I noticed a thought saying: I remember so-and-so who spoke to me rudely."
   This fixed format is not cosmetic — it is what reminds you, every single time, that there is a "you" seeing, and a thought passing.

4. **No judgment. No decorating. No debating.** Do not write "stupid thought" or "nice thought." Do not try to fix anything. You are not in a repair session... you are a watcher on the platform recording train schedules.

5. **Aim to write five to ten thoughts per session.** If more come up — write them too.

6. **And if you find yourself arguing with a thought or trying to convince it that it is wrong** — do not be shaken. That is a thought too. Write it in the format: "I noticed a thought saying: I am defending myself in front of this thought." And continue.

7. **And if you miss a session or two days:** no guilt, no trial. The watcher does not record absence... he records return — one new session, five minutes, and the count continues.

And four common traps — know them from now:
- **"I cannot find any thoughts":** The factory is running in the background and you are not seeing it. Sit a little longer — the first train appears after a minute or two.
- **"The thoughts repeat themselves in writing":** Normal — the repetition is the information itself: "This sentence is coming for the third time" means it is one of the ruling rules... nothing else.
- **"The session makes me more tense":** The first days of the training make life feel noisier — you turned on the light in a room that was dark for years. The noise is not new... it is what was running all along.
- **"I forget to log all day":** Fine — write the moment you remember. The exercise is not a memory test... it is a training drill.

**The sign of success is not that you feel relaxed.** The first sessions are sometimes disturbing — you see the true quantity of thoughts inside you for the first time, and that is not a comforting view. The real sign of success is simpler: that you find yourself, in one of these moments, standing on the platform... watching a train pass — that is all. Not riding it. The first time you feel there is a distance between you and what is happening inside your mind... the Watcher in you is awake.

## Sentence of the Week

"A thought is a suggestion... not an order."

Write it in your phone, and put it in front of you on your desk. All through the coming week, your mind will offer you thousands of suggestions: "Things are hard," "I am tired," "Everyone is better than me," "There is no point." Hear all these suggestions with an open mind — the watcher does not close the platform — but keep the boarding decision... yours. A suggestion was never an order. And the watcher in you is the only one who knows the difference.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

Hisham's ten minutes could be two hours of your life every day. Twenty hours a month. But now you have something Hisham did not have back then: you know there is a platform at all, and you know there is a watcher standing on it.

But let me ask you one final question before we walk together to the next chapter...

So — the trains of thoughts that stop at the platform of your mind... who is sending them in the first place? Who decides that the "worry about money" train arrives exactly while you are trying to sleep? Who keeps the "fear of tomorrow" train running all day long even while you are sitting to rest?

Hisham would have said: my thoughts, from myself. And that is true... but it is not the whole story. Because there is something outside your mind — something with budgets bigger than the budgets of countries — whose only job is to decide what lights up on your screen, and what runs your thought factory while you are not paying attention. Notifications. Screens. "Free" apps. There is an entire economy built on one thing only: your attention.

And in the next chapter we will enter this game from the inside — you will see with your own eyes how it is stolen from you every day without you feeling it... and how to take it back down to the very last minute:

**"Reclaim Your Mind."**
