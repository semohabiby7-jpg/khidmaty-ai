# Chapter Six: I Opened for Others

## The story

Fayez worked in a small workshop making "decorations" for wedding tents.

Seasonal work. Good money in summer, empty in winter. And he complained about the same thing, always: "Every time I bring someone to help me, either he leaves, or he becomes a burden."

Once, in the middle of the season, he had a big order: three weddings in the same week. He brought two young men to help him. One of them, Haitham, was the son of the owner of the iron shop next door.

Fayez was working fourteen hours. And one day, coming back from an installation, he found Haitham standing there looking at his phone and carrying something the wrong way. He shouted at him in front of everyone. Haitham said nothing, put the phone in his pocket, and carried on doing it wrong.

Fayez sat down on a chair, wiped his sweat, and said a sentence he heard from himself for the first time: **"This isn't work. This is exhaustion."**

And then, in the silence, a strange question came to him: **"Did I know how to do this from the start?"**

No. Someone taught him. And how did they teach him? By taking him along step by step, and letting him make mistakes while they stood there.

And Fayez didn't do that with the people around him. He took the work out of their hands and did it himself, saying: "I'll do it faster." That sentence was true in the moment, and stupid over the year.

He was opening the workshop for himself, and closing it on other people.

The story wasn't over. At the end of the season, Haitham left. And before he left he said one sentence: "I learned a lot of things from you, Mr. Fayez... but not from the work. I learned that I don't want to work with someone like you." And he left.

That sentence sat in Fayez's chest for two months. Not because it was unfair — because he knew it was true. He had taught Haitham something, but not the thing he thought he was teaching.

## The box that grows

All through this book we've been talking about a box. And we started with the idea that the box **is not closed**.

And here we reach the last shape of the box: the box that **you** are trying to close on someone else.

Not on purpose. Through "I'll do it faster," and "he's still young," and "he won't know how," and "I'm responsible, I have to be the one who does everything."

And the result: you grow on your own, and you don't grow. The difference between the two is that the first is in your size, and the second is in the size of the people around you.

## How do you know you're closing on people?

There are five signs, and from the inside they all look perfectly normal:

1. **You say "I'll do it faster" more than once a week.** It isn't laziness on anyone's part — you really are faster. But that isn't a cause, it's a result: nobody has been trained.
2. **If you're absent one day, the work stops.** Not slows down — stops. That means all the work is in your head.
3. **You explain the same thing every time, to the same people.** That isn't a lesson, it's proof the work isn't written down.
4. **You do things that aren't your job.** You clear the paperwork, you detail the orders, you fix someone else's mistakes — every day.
5. **Any new person sits with you two weeks and then leaves.** And nobody knows why.

If you find three of the five, don't go looking for a better employee. The work itself is what needs to change.

## Why does the opportunity expand?

There are three practical reasons, not pretty words:

**1) Time.** Everything you do with your own hands takes from your hour. And everything you teach someone turns into an hour that exists.

**2) Repetition.** Work that lives in one head breaks down when that head is busy with something else. Work that is written and taught carries on without you.

**3) The name.** The one who teaches people becomes known. And a name is the cheapest advertising in the world.

And there's a fourth reason nobody says out loud, and for many people it matters more than the three: **rest.**

The one who does everything with his own hands stays gripping. And when you're gripping everything, you can't think about anything new. That's why real expansion doesn't go with gripping. Gripping doesn't just stop you working — it stops you seeing.

## A big difference: training isn't explaining

Most people explain, and think they're teaching.

Explaining delivers information. Training delivers **ability**.

Three things change people:

1. **You do it in front of them** (they see the skill).
2. **You let them do it** (rather than doing it in their place).
3. **You let them get it wrong on something cheap** (a mistake that won't break the work).

And the third part is the one that gets skipped. The reason is understandable: mistakes cost. And people like to prevent cost. But without mistakes, there's no skill.

So the solution is practical: **design the cheap mistake.** Let the person learning get it wrong on something small, at a moment that isn't sensitive, on something that isn't expensive.

An example: in a workshop, the expensive mistake is cutting wood to the wrong measurement. The cheap mistake is cutting old wood, or cutting to a measurement on paper first. Same training, half the cost, a quarter of the pain.

## Five steps that move your work

This is a practical method that works in a workshop, in a shop, in an office, and at home:

**1) Write the work down in steps.** Not in tedious detail. Five to seven big steps. If you can't write them, then the work isn't clear in your head — and that's the first reason nobody can learn it.

**2) Identify the "cheap mistakes."** What can they get wrong without wrecking anything.

**3) Do it once in front of them, with your own hands, telling them why.** Only once. Not ten times. The extra time teaches them to wait, not to learn.

**4) Let them do it, and stay silent.** And this is the hardest item on the list. Silence is hard, but it's the moment they turn from spectator into maker. If you talk halfway through, they go back to watching.

**5) Review after each big step, with one question.** "What could have been better?" Not "what did you do wrong?" The first builds, the second breaks.

## Mistakes that make teaching fail

**Mistake one: you start with the most complicated thing.** People like to teach the thing they're proud of, and it's the hardest thing. Start with the easiest thing that produces a result. The result motivates, and motivation carries on.

**Mistake two: you teach while you're in a hurry.** If you're under pressure, you'll take the work out of their hands. And every time you do that, you teach them not to learn.

**Mistake three: you teach the thing once and think they've learned it.** A skill needs repetition. And repetition needs time that's reserved, not time that's spare.

**Mistake four: you correct their work in front of them without a word.** That's the fastest way to make them leave. If you have to correct it, say why first, and say why it matters.

## And the customer too

The opportunity isn't only in your team. There's an opportunity in your customer.

A customer who brings another customer with him is better than ten adverts. And a customer does that when he understands the value of what he got, not just when he's happy.

There's one question that turns a satisfied customer into a customer who brings others: **"Do you know anyone with the same need?"** One question, at the right time, after he's felt the result.

And the right time isn't the moment of payment. The right time is a week or two later, when he's seen the result of your work in his life. Before that, he's happy with the deal. After that, he's happy with the result. And the difference between the two is the difference between "thank you" and "do you know anyone?"

## When you open, what comes back to you?

People are afraid to teach someone, in case "he takes the work from me."

And that's a real fear, but there are two truths in front of it:

**The first:** the one who takes work because you taught him takes a part of it, and leaves you the part he doesn't know how to do — and that's usually the more valuable part.

**The second:** if you don't teach, the work will exceed your capacity, and the customer will walk away on his own. So the deal is lost either way, but in one of the two cases you gained a person.

And there's a third truth, and it's the most practically important: the one who learns from you stays connected to you by a relationship, not a contract. And a relationship lasts longer than a contract.

## The open box

Notice something beautiful: the box that has other people in it is wider than the box that has only you in it.

Not a metaphor. If you have one person who understands, you now have extra hours, a second pair of eyes, and fewer mistakes. And that's space.

So this part isn't about kindness. It's engineering. Kindness pays in the heart, and engineering pays in space — and you need the space to carry on.

## Another small story: the school that taught in secret

There was a man called Sherif, who ran small photography courses. And he was afraid to teach anyone everything, so he kept the most important part for himself: "face lighting" — the thing that made his photographs different.

And so it went, and people signed up and came out thinking the secret was in the camera. And one of them, a year later, opened a small studio and did good work, but with exactly the same photographs as Sherif. Meaning Sherif earned nothing from his fear, and lost what could have been his.

And once, Sherif made a new course, and put the whole lighting door into it. The result he wasn't expecting: his enrolments tripled, because he became "the one who teaches the thing nobody teaches." And the people who learned from him started sending him big work, because he was "the original."

The fear of teaching charges you twice: once for what you lost, and once for staying where you were because of it.

## The exercise: a handover map

Write down your main work on a sheet of paper, and divide it into three columns:

**1) What I do that nobody can learn quickly** (keep it, and make it deeper).
**2) What I do that someone could learn in a week** (this is what you need to teach).
**3) What I shouldn't have been doing in the first place** (get rid of it, or stop paying for it).

Then answer one question: **"Who is the closest person I could teach column two to?"** Write his name.

And if you don't find a name, then your third column is too big, and you need to clear time first. Teaching needs a place in your day, not a place in your intentions.

## A third story: Amina and the paper in the drawer

Amina worked in a small accounting office, and she was "the one who knows everything."

Every client, every file, every deadline — all of it in her head. And the manager used to praise her: "Nobody in this office understands like Amina." And the praise was real, and it was — without anyone intending it — the strongest lock in her professional life.

She fell ill for a week. And when she came back, she found the files in a mess, two clients upset, and the manager asking her: "How did this happen, when you always hold everything together?"

And she sat thinking: **How? Because I hold everything together.**

So she decided on a small thing, not a "system," not a "platform." She wrote one sheet of paper: **every client, his name, his file, the last deadline, and who calls him.**

One sheet of paper. It went up on the wall.

In the first month, she was going back to it every day and correcting it. In the second month, she split the sheet up: each client on a small card, and the cards in a box, in order. In the third month, she herself didn't need to go back to the box more than twice a week.

And two things happened that Fayez, nor Haitham, nor anyone would have predicted: **it became possible for someone else to work on the files.** And a new person learned in two weeks, and Amina took on more work and more important work. And when she was promoted, there was no crisis, because the work wasn't living inside her.

The sentence she said afterwards was more precise than everything in this book: "I used to think the work in my head was my safety. It turned out the paper was my safety."

What's in your head, you worry about. What's on paper, works without you.

## Delegation isn't handover

There's a difference between two things that most people mix up:

**Delegation:** "Here, do it." — and when he gets it wrong, you take it apart and fix it.
**Handover:** "These are the steps, this is the mistake that's allowed, this is the quality standard, and I'm available at this particular moment." — and when he makes the cheap mistake, you don't step in.

The first makes people hate working with you, and the second makes them grow.

And the difference isn't in the word, it's in the three things that have to exist before you hand any work to anyone:

1. **The written steps.** If they aren't written, he won't learn — he'll guess.
2. **A "done" standard.** How does he know he's finished correctly without asking you? If he doesn't know, he'll ask you every hour, you'll get tired, you'll take the work back into your hands, and you'll start again from the beginning.
3. **The limits of error.** What he's allowed to get wrong, and what is absolutely forbidden.

Those three are simple, and nobody does them. And the result is that everyone with a team in the world says: "I can't find good people." And the reason isn't that there are no good people — it's that nobody knows exactly what's required.

## When do you not teach someone?

There's only one case where you don't teach someone: **when this work is your only advantage, and the whole market is asking for something else.**

But wait — that isn't a justification for not teaching. It's a justification for **holding on to one specific thing**, and teaching the rest. Meaning: keep the thing that, on its own, makes people come to you, and teach everything else.

And the mistake that happens: people hold on to everything, and they hold on to the wrong thing. They hold on to the files and the paperwork and the phone calls, and then they discover it was never an advantage at all — it was just work.

And the question that settles this: **"If the market saw everything I do, what would still make people come to me?"** That answer is what you keep. The rest, teach it.

## How do you teach someone without wrecking your work?

The biggest fear for someone opening the door to others: "I'll teach him, and then find he's taking my work."

The fear isn't imaginary, but the cure isn't to close the door. The cure is to teach in the right way:

**1) Teach the method, not the whole craft.** The craft has years of experience in it and intuition that doesn't transfer through words. The method (the steps, the order, the common mistakes) is what gets learned in a week.

**2) Keep something that stays with you.** The relationship with the client, the name, the decision. What transfers is the execution.

**3) Set the agreement from the start.** "You'll learn this, you'll get this, and you won't get this." Clear words protect the relationship from misunderstanding; they don't wound it.

**4) Test before you expand.** Teach one person on small work, and see the result. Expansion built on one person who carried responsibility and succeeded is safer than ten people trained on paper.

And there's a benefit that's always forgotten: **the person you teach shows you the gaps in your work.** Every question he asks reveals something you do by intuition and can't explain — and that's usually the thing that, if you explained it, you could repeat without yourself.

## The sentence of the week

**The box grows when you let someone else into it... and it doesn't close when you let him try.**
