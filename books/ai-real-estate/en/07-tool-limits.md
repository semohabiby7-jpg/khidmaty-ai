# Chapter 7: The Limits of the Tool — What You Never Hand Over to AI

## The problem

Once you start saving time, the temptation arrives: hand over everything. That is the most dangerous place in this entire book.

The tool writes with confidence, and confidence is not evidence. It can state an estimate as fact, answer a legal question with a sentence that sounds professional and is wrong, or take your client data and keep it somewhere you can't see.

This chapter isn't about fearing technology. It's about **one line** that separates smart use from use that costs you money and reputation.

## The method: five things you never delegate

| Item | Why | The alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial promises** | "12% guaranteed return" is your legal liability, not the tool's | Sourced figures, stated as a range, never a promise |
| **Client personal data** | Names, numbers, addresses and negotiations are not training material | Work with placeholders; strip numbers before pasting |
| **Final formal documents** | Contracts, signed offers, declarations | The tool drafts; you review and sign |
| **Numbers you don't have** | It invents fluently and confidently | Write "not available" in the prompt and ban guessing explicitly |
| **The client's final decision** | Recommendation is your professional duty | The tool presents options; you choose and explain |

### The rule that sums up the chapter

> **The tool writes, you verify, you are accountable.**

Any task that involves a signature, a promise, or a number you're unsure about — the tool stops at the draft.

### Protecting client data in two minutes

1. **Before you paste:** remove full name, phone number, unit number, any ID number.
2. **Replace them:** "Client A", "Unit 1".
3. **Use official work tools** with a clear data policy, and never run client data through anonymous tools.
4. **Afterwards:** delete the conversation if the platform allows. Deleting isn't the same as "no copy exists" — but it beats leaving it there.

### The three-question check

Before anything reaches a client, ask:

1. **Where do these numbers come from?** If not from your own data, cut them.
2. **Is there a promise?** Cut it, or turn it into a description.
3. **If this is wrong, who pays?** If the answer is you, review again.

## The ready prompt: pre-send review

> You are a strict reviewer before content goes to clients. I'll give you a ready-to-send text; review it for:
> 1. Any number or claim that is unsourced or absent from the data I provide.
> 2. Any promise of returns, appreciation, or guarantees.
> 3. Any sentence that could be read as a legal commitment.
> 4. Any personal data that must be removed.
> 5. Any exaggeration that damages credibility.
>
> Then write the clean final version and add one line: "Ready to send" or "Needs your confirmation on [x]".
>
> **Text:** [paste]
> **Verified data:** [the numbers you actually have]

### Before / after

**Before:** "This unit is a guaranteed investment, 12% annual return, and the price will go up before the end of the year."

**After:** "This unit sits in an area with active rental demand. The estimated yield depends on finishing and lease length, and I can show you real numbers from similar units that are actually rented. The current price is 4.2M, and I can't guarantee where the price goes from here."

The second sounds less exciting — and sells better, because the trust it builds doesn't collapse two months later.

## Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Take the last three messages you sent to clients.
2. Look for any promise or unverified number.
3. Rewrite them in the clean version.
4. Create a small file called "Sending Rules" with the five items above.
