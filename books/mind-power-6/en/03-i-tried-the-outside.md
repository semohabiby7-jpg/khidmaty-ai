# Chapter Three: I Tried the Outside

## The story

Huda was certain she was "not one of those people who talk in meetings."

She worked in a big company, and for a year she had been preparing reports that someone else went and presented. She'd spend a week analysing numbers and produce a clean report, and then a colleague would walk into the meeting and present the work, and come back and tell her "it was excellent, people liked it." And every time, she'd smile and say "praise be to God" — and sit at her desk with a lump in her throat she couldn't name.

Once she told me: "I do the work, and someone else takes the talking."

I asked her: "What's the smallest thing you could do that contradicts that sentence?"

She said: "I don't understand."

"The smallest thing. Not a meeting. Not a presentation. Something so small you'd feel it doesn't even count."

She thought. Then said, carefully: "Maybe I could write one line in the team chat about a number I found?"

"Great. One line. For seven days. One line a day. And if there's no number, one line of observation."

Huda did it. Seven days, seven lines in the chat. No meeting, no microphone, no faces looking at her, no one cutting her off.

Day one, she wrote the line and then sat staring at the screen for ten minutes.

Day two, she wrote it faster.

Day three, she wrote a line about something nobody had asked her for.

Day four, someone replied: "Important information, thank you."

Day five, someone else asked her a question in the chat, and she answered.

Day six, the manager asked: "Were you the one who wrote that number? Can you break it down?"

And Huda broke it down. Also in the chat. And when she finished, she sat looking at the conversation and said to herself: "I did that?"

Two weeks later, the manager told her: "We need you to present that part next week."

For the first time in her life, she presented. And she fumbled. And she forgot a line in the middle. And she drank some water. And two minutes later she carried on normally.

And she finished. And nobody died. And nobody laughed.

And Huda didn't change in seven days. **The sentence** is what broke, because evidence arrived that she couldn't deny.

But there's a small detail in this story, and if you miss it you'll miss the most important lesson. On day one, Huda wasn't brave. She was terrified, she wrote the line with a trembling hand, and she sat ten minutes waiting for a catastrophic reaction that never came. The courage didn't come before the experiment; it came after it, when she saw that the world didn't collapse.

## Why is experience stronger than talk?

Talk goes through a filter. Experience goes through reality.

When someone tells you "you're more capable than you think," your brain answers at rocket speed: "that's motivational talk." And nothing changes. Because talk never touches the part that holds the sentence. Talk reaches the part that agrees and says "yeah, true," and then walks away.

But when you do something small and see a result with your own eyes, the brain has no answer. That result is **evidence**, and evidence doesn't need permission.

And if your brain tries to play the result down ("they were just being nice," "it was easy anyway," "someone helped me") — the answer is simple: repeat the experiment, differently, and see if the explanation holds. One piece of evidence can be reinterpreted. Ten, hardly.

And the difference between the two is that experience works on level three of the belief — the identity level — because it says: "Look, you did it. So the definition in your head isn't complete."

And something important here: **the experiment doesn't need to succeed in order to work.** It needs to happen. Even if the result was mediocre, it breaks the sentence "impossible." Impossible doesn't require success; it requires one time only.

And there's another difference, which explains why people read self-development books and stay exactly where they are: reading gives you **meaning**, experience gives you **memory**. And in critical moments the brain doesn't reach for meanings, it reaches for memories. In the moment your hand hesitates before asking for something, you won't remember a line from a book. You'll remember that you asked once before and nothing bad happened. That's what will stand with you.

## Why does the brain resist the experiment?

Before we get to the law, we have to admit something: the brain isn't stupid. It knows the experiment is dangerous.

And the danger isn't necessarily a real danger — it's an **accounting** danger. The brain calculates: if the sentence is right, and the experiment fails, what do I lose? And there you find all the fears stacked up: an image of myself that's been going for years, people who see me a certain way, a way of dealing with my family and my work that's already in motion.

So practically, the experiment isn't a small step. The experiment is an **announcement** that the old story isn't final. And any announcement like that needs courage — even a small amount.

Which is why the first condition in the law that follows is "small" — not to fool ourselves, but to bring the cost of failure down to something acceptable. An experiment whose failure is expensive dies before it's born.

## The law of the small experiment

The experiment must be:

**1) Very small.** Something you can do while tired, and something nobody will comment on. If it needs big courage, it's too big to start with. And that's not a flaw. Courage is built, not born complete.

The practical test: if the experiment requires you to feel ready, it's too big. The right experiment gets done while you're not ready, not comfortable, and have other work to do.

**2) Specific.** Not "I'll talk more." "I'll write one line in the chat every day." General sentences get lost in the crowd; specific ones get executed.

And the reason is simple: a general sentence has no moment of execution. "I'll talk more" — when? There is no when. "I'll write a line every day at 11" has a moment, and that moment gets accounted for.

**3) Time-boxed.** Seven days. Not a month, not "from today on." A short duration lowers the fear and makes failure cheap. And anything time-boxed is easier to start, because your brain knows there's an end.

**4) Recorded.** Not in your head. In a note. Because the brain deletes small successes and keeps failures. Paper doesn't delete. And that's not a metaphor — it's a real difference in recall.

And if you record it by mood, the record becomes moody. So the record has to be dry: date, what you did, the result. No commentary on the same day. Commentary at the end of the week.

**5) Measured by a result, not a feeling.** Not "I felt better." "I wrote 7 lines, and two people replied." Feelings shift with mood, coffee, and sleep; results stay.

**6) Contains a dose of fear.** And I put this last because it matters most: it has to be something you're a little afraid of. If the experiment is completely comfortable, it isn't an experiment — it's an old habit, performed calmly. The dose required is small, but it has to be there.

## The smallest possible step

Every belief has a microscopic version of what you're afraid of. Look for that version:

| The belief | The real fear | The smallest experiment |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm not a talker" | Someone answers me and embarrasses me | One written line in a chat, once a day |
| "I can't sell" | Someone says "no" to my face | Ask one person about a price, with nothing to sell |
| "I can't learn" | Looking slow | A 15-minute video + two notes |
| "I won't find an opportunity" | Getting demoralised | Read 3 listings and write one note about each |
| "I have no right to ask" | Looking greedy | Ask someone safe for something tiny (water, information, time) |
| "I can't speak in front of a group" | Fumbling | Ask one question in a meeting of seven people |
| "I can't talk on the phone" | Fumbling and going silent | A one-minute call to book an appointment, then hang up |
| "Money makes me uncomfortable" | Looking greedy | Say your price clearly, once, without explaining |

The idea isn't just to practise the skill. The idea is to **submit evidence** against the sentence.

And the difference between the two is large: practising the skill makes you better at the thing. Submitting evidence makes you see yourself differently. You need both — but the order matters: evidence first, because without it you'll be learning while not believing you're learning.

And a third thing happens if you do the experiment right: **the thing that was big gets smaller.** The meeting that was a mountain, after three lines, became an ordinary thing. Not because the meeting changed, but because your brain saw it up close and learned its details. And fear lives in distance.

## What if you fail?

This is where the whole book is decided.

If you tried and the result was bad, there are two interpretations. One confirms the sentence; one widens it.

**The failure that confirms:** "See? Told you. I'm not a person for this." — This interpretation uses the situation as final evidence, and shuts the door.

**The failure that widens:** "I failed at a specific thing, in a specific circumstance, for a specific reason. What's the reason?" — This interpretation uses the situation as information, and leaves the door open.

The difference isn't in the situation. It's in the question you ask after it.

One question separates them: **"What was missing, that I could have done?"** Not "why did I fail?" (that question drowns you). "What was missing?" (that question is useful).

And if the reason was genuinely outside you, write it down. The external reason is part of the truth, not an excuse. And the complete truth is more useful than the truth that blames you.

And one more thing has to be said: **failing at the experiment isn't failing at the book.** The experiment didn't happen — fine. What actually fails is interpreting one experiment as a verdict on an entire life.

And there's a fourth kind of outcome, very common and never discussed: **the experiment that works and you don't believe it.** You write the lines, people reply, and something in you says: "the circumstances were easy." That isn't humility — that's the sentence defending itself with its last weapon.

The answer to that isn't argument. The answer is to change the circumstance. Try in another place, with other people, on another subject. If the sentence still says "circumstances," then the circumstances have become too many to explain away.

## Seven days... and then?

After the seven days, sit for ten minutes and do three things:

**1) Read the record.** What actually happened, in numbers, not in feeling.

**2) Write one sentence:** "What happened was different from what I expected in ______."

**3) Decide the next step.** Not a project, not a one-year plan. Another experiment, slightly bigger.

And if the seven days didn't happen — and they often don't — don't start over. Start from the middle: do the experiment for three days only. Failure to commit is usually not weak will; it's an experiment that was too big to start with.

And here's practical advice that saves years: **attach the experiment to something you already do.** Huda already read the chat every day at work. So the experiment didn't ask for a new habit; it asked for one line inside an existing one. Experiments that demand a whole new system die on day three.

## The experiment isn't a luxury

There's a common idea that experimenting is for people with time and money. That's wrong.

The experiment is the cheapest thing in the world. The most expensive thing is the years that pass while you think.

One man spends ten years saying "I'll start when I'm ready," and another spends ten years trying something every month. The same ten years pass for both. Only one of them has evidence.

And there's another difference between them, the important one: the first has **a plan**, the second has **knowledge**. A plan changes the moment it touches reality; knowledge accumulates.

## Exercise: the seven-day test

Take a sheet of paper and draw seven boxes.

**At the top:** write the sentence you're going to contradict, and the precise version you wrote in chapter two.

**At the bottom:** define the experiment in one specific sentence containing a verb, a number, and a duration.

**In the seven boxes:** every day, write the date, exactly what you did, and the result you saw.

And at the end of day seven, write one line: "The evidence I have now is ______."

Then send that line to yourself as a message, or photograph it, or put it in your notes. So that when the old sentence comes back in a month — and it will come back — you have paper.

## Why is a small experiment stronger than advice?

Advice goes through the filter. You hear something nice, you say "true," and a week later you've forgotten it. Because advice addresses the conscious mind, and the belief lives somewhere else.

Experience goes through no filter. It happened. It was touched. You have evidence now, and evidence isn't forgotten as easily.

That's why the book says "try seven days," not "be convinced." You don't need to be convinced of something new; you need to do something small and see.

And there's another benefit to the small experiment: **it shows you the truth without costing you much.** If the old sentence was right, you lose a week. If it was wrong, you gain years.

## When do you stop the experiment?

An important question nobody answers: you're experimenting — when do you know the experiment failed?

Only three cases count as real failure:

**1) It wasn't an experiment to begin with.** "I'll try to be social" isn't an experiment. "I'll talk to 3 people at work this week" is.

**2) You did it for one day and stopped.** That isn't the experiment failing; that's you stopping the experiment.

**3) You did it properly, and the result said "no."** That isn't failure — that's **information**. And information is a success, even when it looks like a loss.

Everything else isn't failure, it's adjustment: the timing was wrong, the size was too big, the wrong person, the method needs changing.

## The "seven days, not seventy" rule

There's a mistake that keeps repeating: people take the idea and expand it. "Seven days" becomes "a month," and "a small step" becomes "a full plan." And a week later, the big plan collapses, and they say "I tried and failed."

The rule: **take the smallest version of the experiment, and do it for 7 days.** If the 7 days pass without doing it, the experiment is too big. Make it smaller again — don't postpone it.

## The sentence of the week

**A sentence changes when you find evidence, and evidence isn't born from thinking.**
