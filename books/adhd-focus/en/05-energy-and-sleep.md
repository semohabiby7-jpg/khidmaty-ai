# Chapter Five: Energy and Sleep — When to Work, When to Stop

## The problem

There's a fantasy we grow without noticing: that work is available 24 hours a day, and the only problem is that you "don't start".

So you stand trial in the hours when you're completely empty. Eleven at night, after a full day, and your mind can't find a single word. Then you tell yourself: "I'm a failure, I should have done this." That isn't failure — that's **accounting at the wrong hour**.

Body and mind run on a schedule. Attention isn't a power grid left on until you cut it. It's a wave. Some hours your mind is sharp, some it's mud. And the gap isn't small — it can be threefold.

## The core idea: there is no "no time", there is "wrong time"

Take the same task and place it in your sharp hour and it finishes in half the time. Leave it in your mud hour and it takes three times as long, with more fatigue and a worse result.

You don't need more hours. You need **the right task at the right hour**.

## First: know your sharp hour

Two simple questions:
1. **When do you find yourself thinking fast?** Not "when are you free" — when is your mind present.
2. **When do you read a line three times and still not get it?** That's your mud hour.

People usually get this wrong. They assume the sharp hour is early morning. In reality it varies: some are good in the morning, some after noon, some at night. **Don't borrow someone else's schedule.** Take one week and log one line a day: best hour, worst hour. After a week your pattern is clear.

## The mental day (three kinds of hours)

| Type | How it feels | Good for | Not for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sharp** | you grasp fast, ideas arrive | thinking work: writing, analysis, decisions | quick replies, admin |
| **Medium** | moving, but slowly | repetitive: email, tidying, follow-ups | deep thinking |
| **Mud** | can't hold focus for 5 minutes | mechanical: organising files, clearing, sorting | anything needing a brain |

The most common mistake: spending your sharp hour on email and your mud hour on the most important work. Then saying "I can't focus". Of course you can't — you're trying to sprint during a stroll.

**Rule:** the first sharp hour of your day is never for replies.

## Energy isn't just sleep

Four things set your energy, and each one hits the others:

1. **Sleep** — the base. Short on it and nothing else works.
2. **Food** — not grand "healthy eating". Just don't be empty all day and get ambushed by hunger at four.
3. **Movement** — if your body sits for six hours, your mind sits with it.
4. **Mental rest** — time when your mind isn't being demanded of.

Break one and the other three run at half capacity. Which is why "I can't work" is often not an attention problem — it's an **energy** problem.

## Sleep: not a luxury, a setting

Your brain cleans itself and sorts the day while you sleep. At five hours you're not just tired — you're **running on an unfiltered mind**.

You don't need a miracle schedule. Three things make a real difference:
1. **A fixed wake time** — more important than bedtime. Fix the waking and sleep sorts itself.
2. **An end-of-day signal** — something that tells your brain "work is over": close the laptop, clear the desk, change clothes. Without it, your brain stays in work mode and sleep arrives late.
3. **The phone is not the first thing before sleep or after waking** — those two half-hours are the most expensive in your day.

## The problem nobody mentions: late sleep

Many people with focus difficulties sleep late — and not from laziness. Because **night is the only time with no interruption**. The world is quiet, nobody wants anything, and finally your mind works.

If that's you, the fix isn't forcing an early night. The fix is building **an interruption-free gap in the daytime** — one closed, known, protected hour. Find quiet in the day and the night loses its only job.

## When to stop

An important idea: **the end of the day is not decision time.** When tired, your brain inflates problems and shrinks solutions. Anything you decide in the mud looks harder than it is.

So: **decisions belong in the sharp hour, not the end of the day.** And even then, draft them and review in the morning.

## The clean stop

Leave in a way that makes returning easy. Three things before you close:
1. **Write the next step** in one line.
2. **Leave the file open** in the same place.
3. **Write one sentence**: "where I stopped, and what's next".

One minute saves you fifteen tomorrow while wondering "what was I doing?"

## Mud is not a failure signal

Some days your mind is mud from morning. You don't have to fight it. **Change the type of work, not the amount.** On a mud day: clean up files, answer old messages, tidy the desk, make simple calls. None of that needs a sharp mind.

The point is that the day doesn't end at zero. A mud day with mechanical work beats a mud day of self-blame a hundred times over.

## Waves, not marathons

Even in your sharp hour you can't work four hours straight. Your mind works best in waves: **45–50 minutes on, 10 off**, and a longer break after three waves.

One point matters more than the waves themselves:

> **The phone is not rest.**

Scrolling burns the same part of your mind you're trying to rest and leaves you more tired than before. Real rest: get up, drink water, look out the window, walk five minutes, or close your eyes.

The difference between rest that restores you and rest that robs you is one thing: **your hand isn't holding a screen.**

## A note

This is general guidance on daily habits — not diagnosis or treatment. If you have an ongoing sleep problem, consistently wake unrefreshed, or suspect a sleep disorder, that needs a professional, and this book isn't a substitute.

## Before / after

**Before:** wakes, opens the phone, answers messages, spends the sharp hour on email, then tries to write the most important thing after noon while exhausted. Result: procrastination and blame.

**After:** wakes, one closed sharp hour on the most important task, messages after, mud for tidying. Result: same day, same person, different work.

## Chapter rule

> You're not a 24-hour machine. You're a wave. Use the peak, be kind to the trough.

## Chapter exercise (one week, 2 minutes a day)

Each night, write three lines only:
1. **Best hour** today (when you were sharp).
2. **Worst hour**.
3. **One task** you'll place in tomorrow's sharp hour.

After seven days you'll see a clear pattern — and that pattern beats any ready-made schedule, because it's **yours**.
