# Chapter One: I Determined

There's a first question — older than all the others, and the most deceptive: when you stand at the starting point of this part and ask: "Where was I going?"

Not "Where am I going now?" — but "Where was I going, back when I set out?" Because the first step after arrival isn't measuring what you reached. It's making sure you were heading where you chose to go in the first place. And the frightening truth: many of us reach the end of a road... and discover it wasn't our road.

And we'll begin as we always begin... with a story.

## The Story of Tarek and the Borrowed Destination

Tarek is forty-two. A general manager in a large company. On paper, successful. A good salary, a respected title, people who report to him. Everything around him says: "This man arrived."

But Tarek has a secret he doesn't share with anyone. When he sits alone — in his office after everyone leaves, or in his car before going home — he feels something he can't name. Not sadness. Not regret. Something quieter: a feeling that he's walking a path... that isn't his.

The story begins years ago. Tarek was a young man unsure what to study. His father said: "Business administration. There's money in it, and respect." His uncle said: "Management. Our family are managers, not engineers." And Tarek... listened. Not because he agreed. Because he didn't know what he wanted. And when you don't know what you want, the easiest thing is to take what others choose for you.

He studied. He graduated. He worked. He got promoted. He climbed. Every step on the ladder was correct — on paper. And every step took him further from something he never named.

Years later, Tarek sat with himself one night and asked: "If I had chosen for myself — without my father, without my uncle, without anyone — would I be here?" The answer that came was quiet and clear: "No."

Not because management is bad. Not because his father was wrong. But because the destination was borrowed. It wasn't Tarek's. It was his father's dream, his uncle's vision, society's definition of success — placed on Tarek's shoulders when he was too young to carry his own.

That night, Tarek asked himself a question that changed his life:

**"Am I going where I chose to go... or where someone else chose for me?"**

## The Compass and the Ship

Imagine a ship. A strong ship, well-built, with a skilled crew and a powerful engine. Everything is ready. But the compass — the device that tells the ship which direction is north — is broken. Not broken in an obvious way. It still points. It still spins. It looks like it's working. But it points south when the ship thinks it's going north.

What happens to this ship? It moves. It sails. The engine runs. The crew works. The ship crosses the sea. Everything looks normal from the outside. But the ship is heading in the wrong direction. And the longer it sails, the further it gets from where it intended to go.

That's exactly what happens with a borrowed destination. You move. You work. You achieve. But the compass inside you — the one that should tell you where your true north is — is set to someone else's north. And the further you go, the further you get from yourself.

The frightening thing about this: you don't feel lost. You feel like you're going somewhere. Because you are going somewhere — just not where you should be. And the only way to know is to stop, hold the compass, and ask: "Whose north is this?"

## The Idea in Points

### First: Not Every Destination Is Yours

There's a difference between a destination you chose and a destination that was chosen for you. Both look the same from the outside: both are goals, both have plans, both take effort. But one is yours and the other is borrowed. And a borrowed destination — no matter how noble, no matter how profitable — will never feel like yours. Because it isn't.

Tarek didn't fail. He succeeded at someone else's goal. And that's the cruelest kind of success: one that looks right from the outside and feels wrong from the inside. Because the compass is pointing somewhere else.

### Second: "I Don't Know What I Want" Is the Beginning, Not the End

Most people who walk a borrowed path didn't choose it out of weakness. They chose it because they didn't know what they wanted. And "I don't know what I want" isn't a flaw — it's a starting point. The problem is when you stop there and let others fill in the blank.

The right move when you don't know what you want isn't to take someone else's answer. It's to stay with the question. To search. To try. To ask yourself: "If no one was watching, if no one would judge, if money wasn't the issue — what would I do?" The answer might not come immediately. But it will come. And when it does, it will be yours.

### Third: You Can Change Your Destination at Any Age

Tarek was forty-two when he asked the question. Not twenty-two. Not thirty-two. Forty-two. And that wasn't too late. It's never too late to correct your compass. Because the alternative — walking the wrong road for another twenty years — is worse.

Changing your destination doesn't mean quitting everything tomorrow. It means starting to steer. A small course correction. A class. A conversation. A side project. The ship doesn't need to turn 180 degrees. It needs to adjust its heading. And a small adjustment, sustained over time, takes you to a completely different place.

### Fourth: The Real Destination Is "Who," Not "Where"

Here's the deeper truth: your destination isn't a place. It's a version of you. "Who do I want to become?" is a more powerful question than "Where do I want to go?" Because places change. Titles change. Jobs change. But who you become — that stays.

Tarek wasn't asking "Where should I work?" He was asking "Who do I want to be?" And when he answered that, the "where" started to rearrange itself. That's the secret: get the "who" right, and the "where" follows.

## The Chapter Exercise: The "I Determined" Page

**What you need:** A piece of paper, a pen, and ten quiet minutes.

1. **At the top, write:** "My destination"
   Write what you think your current destination is. In one sentence. Where are you heading?

2. **Below that, write:** "Who chose it?"
   Be honest. Was it you? Your parents? Society? Fear? Money? Write the name.

3. **Ask yourself:** "If no one was watching, what would I do?"
   Write the answer. Even if it's vague. Even if it scares you. Write it.

4. **Ask the final question:** "Whose destination is this — mine or borrowed?"
   If it's yours: you're on the right road. If it's borrowed: you now know. And knowing is the first step to correcting the compass.

## The Sentence of the Week

**"A borrowed destination takes you far... from yourself."**

Write it and keep it in front of you. All week, whenever you feel that quiet unease — that feeling that something is off — ask yourself: "Whose north am I following?" If it's not yours, it's time to adjust the compass.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

You now know where you were heading. But knowing where you were going is only the first question. The next question is deeper: you set out, you walked the road, you arrived — but what did you actually reach? Because sometimes you arrive... and what you reached isn't what you think. That's the next chapter.
