# Chapter Three: The Words You Say to Yourself

We came back from Karim's story with something gained: there is an entire economy in this world built on your attention... and you saw its bill for the first time. You won the round that was against something outside your head.

But the next round is harder. Because its opponent is not outside. Its opponent lives inside, uses your own voice, and says things you have been used to for years... to the point that you do them without even hearing them.

## The Story of Omar and the Narrator in His Head

He is thirty-one years old, and he runs a small shop-fitting business: decor, electrical work, and finishing. After a year and a half of hard work and saving, the opportunity of a lifetime appeared: a big investor saw the idea of Omar's new project — a small app connecting skilled workers with people — and wanted to hear more. The meeting was Sunday morning.

The night before the meeting, Omar sat at his desk reviewing the numbers. The numbers are good. The project is solid. But every time he looked at the paper, he found — there is no radio playing inside his head... no, there IS a radio playing inside his head:

"These numbers will not impress him... they only look good to you."
"Investors like this meet people bigger than you."
"Your words will get tangled... they always get tangled in the big moments."
"And watch out for the profits question... you are terrible at mental math."

He resisted: "Then why am I even here?" So the radio answered: "So you can watch yourself lose the opportunity."

Omar closed the file and fell asleep on that voice.

In the morning, ten minutes before the meeting, standing in front of the place, he heard himself say a sentence out loud without intending to:

"Here we go, Omar... today you are right on schedule to prove you are a failure."

He froze. That sentence was not from the numbers, not from the presentation, not from anything on the paper. It was from inside him.

He walked into the meeting. He spoke well... relatively. But the man who entered that hall was not a man coming to sell an idea — he was a man coming to apologize for his existence. His voice dropped lower than it should. He agreed to changes he knew were wrong. And he left without getting a follow-up... and the investor thanked him with perfect politeness, with the sentence Omar knows by heart too: "We'll think about it and get back to you."

In the car on the way back, the radio opened: "Told you, Omar... this never wins you anything."

But this time Omar suddenly asked a question he had never asked himself: this sentence... where did I bring it from?

And in the moment of stopping at the red light, the flash came: Omar is nine years old, standing next to his father at work, breaking a glass cup, and his father shouting: "You never succeed at anything!" — twenty-one years have passed since that scene... and the narrator in Omar's head is still replaying the same cassette.

In that moment Omar understood something that shook an idea about himself he had held for years: the voice speaking inside his head... is not his voice at all. It is an old recording, planted when he was small, replaying for twenty-one years... and all his life Omar thought it was "his opinion of himself."

Three weeks later, Omar pitched to other people. Same project. Same numbers. Practically the same man. With one small difference: before going in, Omar had done something with the radio in his head — the exercise you will learn in a few lines. He walked into the meeting... and the investor took his number.

And the story does not say Omar became a millionaire — Omar is still halfway there. The story says something simpler and more important: one project, one set of numbers, one man... and the man is the only thing that changed.

## The Idea in Points

### First: There is a radio playing in your head all day... and you do not own the switch

The inner dialogue is not passing thoughts — it is a running commentary on your life, broadcast from the moment you wake until you sleep: it judges your look in the mirror, reviews your meal, gets angry at a driver, mocks you when you make a mistake, and doubts you before anyone else does. Thousands of sentences a day.

The problem is not that the radio is playing — that is the operating system we all run on. The problem is that its voice became "the voice of reality": from hearing it so much, we now call any sentence it says "me" and "my nature." And you never asked yourself Omar's question: this sentence... who wrote it? And when? And is it even true?

### Second: You talk to yourself in a way you would never let anyone talk to your closest friend

A moment of honesty right now: if someone — anyone — walked up to your dearest friend and said to him: "You never succeed at anything"... "Don't pitch it, they will see right through you"... "This is too big for you"...

Would you stay silent? No. You would stand in his face. There is no way you would let anyone talk to your friend like that.

The sentences we accept for ourselves every day — in the mirror, on the commute, after any mistake — if they were said to our friend, the whole world would have to answer. So why, when the narrator says them from inside... do we surrender?

The answer is small and scary: because he says them in our own voice. The one who speaks, the one being spoken to, and the one who hears... are all in the same room, and there is no witness outside.

### Third: These words are written... and not one sentence of them is carved in iron

And here is the good news. The sentences that repeat in your head did not come from your genes — they are sentences that were written: by a moment like Omar's glass cup, or an old teacher's comment, or a comparison, or mockery from a schoolmate long ago. They were written by experience... and everything that was written can be edited.

The difference between "I can't" and "I'll try and see" is not mere optimism... it is swapping the operating instruction. And the recording that was made when you were nine gets edited by sentences being written in your life right now — when they are repeated more than the old ones.

And the golden rule of editing: do not lie to yourself. "I am the greatest businessman in Egypt" — your mind will throw it out, because it knows it is not honest. The right alternative is a true, believable sentence: "I am well prepared... and I have tried and succeeded before." Editing is not illusion — editing is honesty with yourself.

### Fourth: When does the radio get loud?

The radio does not run at the same volume all day. There are three hours when the static peaks, and in them most of us believe our biggest lies about ourselves:

- **Before sleep:** the end of the day, the battery is drained, and no one is checking the radio. Sentences at this hour dig deeper than at any other time — that is why you toss and turn in bed over an old moment... and you are not tossing over the moment itself, you are tossing over its re-broadcast.
- **After any stumble:** the moment you make a mistake or fall, the radio runs at full volume and issues final verdicts on your entire life from a single stumble. This is not analysis... it is a live broadcast of the old program.
- **In comparisons:** the moment you open social media, the radio takes the highlight reel of someone else's life and turns it into commentary on your own — with old voices and new confidence.

The solution is not silencing the radio during these hours — that is nearly impossible. The solution is that you know the appointment itself: "It is one in the morning... and this is the static hour. Anything said right now is not binding." The sentence that gets carved in at midnight gets reopened in the morning light... and shows its true face. Omar's rule for the meeting was not "silence the radio" — it was "the radio does not rule during the static hour."

## Chapter Exercise: Editing the Words

One week, and a paper and pen. The foundation is the Watcher Training you did in the first chapter — this time on one specific kind of train: words.

1. **One day of logging the radio.** Exactly like the Watcher Training, but the format changes: instead of "I noticed a thought saying"... write "I heard myself saying: ...". No judgment of the sentence — logging only. Five to ten sentences over the day.

2. **Pinpoint the one ruling sentence.** Read what you wrote, and choose the sentence that repeats the most and hurts the most. You will usually find it short, and starting with "I". That is the one we will work on.

3. **Write its realistic replacement.** Not a fantasy dream — a sentence of honesty: "I never get anything right" → "I get many things right, and what I get right takes more than one try." "I can't present this" → "I will prepare well... and try." The replacement gets written on a small paper in front of you, and becomes your phone wallpaper.

4. **A week of swapping.** Every time you hear the old sentence — and you will hear it — say the replacement immediately, in a low voice or in your head. Do not wait to feel confident first... the sentence is what brings the feeling, not the feeling that brings the sentence. And at the end of each day, put a mark on the paper for every swap you made.

5. **And if you need a starting point... these are the five most famous sentences on the radio, and their realistic replacements:**
   - "I look terrible today" → "This is my look today... and today is not the measure of my life."
   - "I made a mistake... I always make mistakes" → "I made a mistake this time... and my whole life still has plenty right in it."
   - "Everyone is better than me" → "I am watching their highlight reel... and my reel is being edited too."
   - "I am tired of myself" → "I want to change one thing in my day... and it should be very small."
   - "I can never succeed at anything" → "I have not tried enough times yet to judge."

   Notice: every replacement is not a dream or a goal... it is a realistic reply. And that is the acceptance condition your mind sets — if the replacement gets rejected, shrink it until it is accepted.

**The sign of success:** it is not that the old sentence dies — old sentences never fully die. The sign of success is that it drops in rank: from "a fact about me"... to "a passing memory." And the first time you hear it and the replacement comes to you automatically, without thinking — the editing has started writing itself.

## Sentence of the Week

"Instead of 'I can't'... 'I'll try and see.'"

Do not wait until you are ready to say it — say it while you are not ready, while you are afraid, while you are unsure. This is not a magic sentence... it is the smallest decision that can be made in a moment of fear. And small decisions are the ones that make it through.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

You now own the first thing you need for real change to begin: you can see the narrator inside, you have heard him say "I" while he is not you, and you know his words are paper... and paper can be written again.

But there is a logical question anyone who does this exercise seriously must face: but why? Why does a repeated sentence gain this much power? Who gave ordinary words — letters on the wind — the authority to dig into your mind?

And the answer is stranger and more beautiful than you imagine: your mind does not ask "is this sentence right or wrong"... your mind asks one question only: "has it been repeated enough, or not?"

And this is the most dangerous rule in the whole book. Whoever understands it will understand why fear grows out of repeated sentences, why some people's dreams come true without them knowing why, and why the one who says "I am tired" every day... feels genuinely tired.

This rule is the next chapter, and I am not exaggerating when I tell you it is the key to everything that follows:

**"Your mind believes what gets repeated."**
