# Chapter One: I Saw the Box

## The story

Mostafa worked in a warehouse.

Seven years. Same place, same shelves, same paperwork. Same sound of the machine locking the door at nine, same smell of cardboard that had become the smell of his life. And he always said one thing about himself — always with a laugh: "I'm not a talker."

He said it when someone suggested he apply for a supervisory post. He said it when his manager asked why he never showed up to the weekly meeting. He said it when his wife told him, "Talk to the owner about a raise — you're the longest-serving man he's got." He said it like that, with a laugh, as if it were a fact about the world, like the sun coming up in the morning.

And that laugh is the most important thing in the whole story.

Because if he had said it sadly, someone would have asked why. If he had said it angrily, someone would have been offended. But the laugh protects the sentence from being questioned. The laugh says: I'm fine like this, don't bother.

Then one time, something very small happened. Small enough that anyone could have forgotten it.

The night shift ran late. The manager was travelling, and the driver who was supposed to receive the shipment called to say he was stuck on the agricultural road. And the big client — a man who took half the warehouse every month — came himself, standing at the door, seeing with his own eyes that the order wasn't ready the way it was supposed to be.

And nobody was there except Mostafa.

He stopped. Wiped his hands on his trousers. Opened the paperwork. And started explaining the order to the client, item by item: what had been loaded, what was short, when it would be finished, why that particular item was late (because the supplier had sold it to another factory), and what substitute could work in its place without losing a day.

Fifteen minutes of talking.

No written sheet. No stammering. No "sorry" and no "the thing is" and no "actually that's not my department." And he was answering the client's questions before the client finished asking them.

When he was done, the client was quiet for a moment. He drank from the water bottle in his hand. And said one sentence:

"Why aren't you in the office?"

Mostafa laughed. The old laugh. "I'm not a talker."

The client gave him a strange look — the look of a man who has just seen something he didn't expect — and said: "What I heard from you just now, there isn't a single office I deal with that has it. And I've been dealing with offices for twenty years."

And he left.

Mostafa went home and couldn't sleep.

Not because of the praise. Praise flies and fades. What kept him awake was something else: **the first time he heard his own voice from the outside.**

He had never heard himself talk like that. He had always heard the sentence, not the voice. And for seven years, the sentence had been narrating him better than his voice could. In those fifteen minutes, the voice overtook the sentence, and what lay underneath it showed.

## What happened that night

Mostafa had lived inside a sentence for seven years, and he had never once looked at it.

Not because he was stupid. Because the sentence worked very well: every time someone asked him for something big, the sentence arrived before he could think, convinced him he didn't deserve it, and relieved him of the pain of trying. And at the end, it made him laugh.

The sentence never stood in front of him and said "you're a failure." It said "you don't need this." And that's more dangerous.

Not laziness. Not weakness. **A belief.** A belief that protects its owner from fear, and at the same time keeps him from his own life. Like a door you put up against the wind, then forget you're behind it for a year.

What happened that night was that the sentence broke for a moment — not because someone persuaded him, but because he saw himself from a distance. He saw himself doing the thing he says he doesn't do.

A month later, Mostafa tried something for the first time in his life: he put his name on a supervisory job application.

He was rejected. Of course he was rejected. He told himself: "See? Told you."

And the sentence came back happy, and put its clothes on again.

But something had changed, and nobody saw it: rejection stopped being evidence. It became just a result.

Before, rejection used to say to him: "See? You really aren't a talker." Now rejection said: "That application didn't work." A small difference in words, and a huge one in a life.

Two months later he tried again, and got an answer. That's not our subject. Our subject is that the sentence lost its authority the moment it became **visible** — the moment he could say, "ah, that's the sentence that scares me," instead of "that's the truth."

## So what is a belief?

Let me put it in the simplest form, and please read it twice:

**A belief is a sentence about yourself, repeated so many times that it no longer needs evidence.**

Doesn't need evidence. That's the key word.

If you told someone "Cairo is the capital of Egypt," he wouldn't ask who said so. The information has settled. A belief is exactly like that. It's no longer an idea you question; it's ground you stand on.

The point isn't that it's necessarily wrong. It might be true, it might be false, it might be half and half. The problem isn't its accuracy. The problem is that it **rules without being questioned.**

A man who is certain he's "not a talker" will stay silent until he dies. Not because talking is hard, but because the sentence decides for him before he tries. He never made the decision to stay silent. The silence was decided for him.

And here's a small distinction that changes everything:

- **An opinion:** I don't like speaking in front of people. (A state — it changes)
- **A belief:** I'm not one of those people who speaks. (An identity — it closes)

The first describes a moment. The second describes a fate.

And the difference between them isn't in the words. It's in what comes after. A person with an opinion can try, because his opinion is allowed to change. A person with a belief doesn't try, because trying has become a threat to himself rather than a test.

## How is a belief built? (Briefly — we'll take it apart in the next chapter)

Just four components. Four — I said three in the first draft and it turned out incomplete, so let's say it properly:

**1) A sentence.** Someone said it at a sensitive moment.

**2) Repetition.** It was said again, or you said it to yourself again.

**3) Fear.** The first time you went against it, something bad happened, or you were afraid it would.

**4) A payoff.** The sentence gave you something. It protected you from embarrassment, from extra work, from disappointment. And as long as it gives you something, you'll be afraid to drop it.

Sentence + repetition + fear + payoff = fact.

And after years, the fact becomes part of you, and nobody asks who said it.

## The common boxes

Not everyone is in the same box, but the boxes resemble each other. Seven types you'll see every day — see which one you're in, and don't rush.

**1) The capability box.** "I can't." Not "I don't know how" — "I can't." "I don't know how" opens a door to learning; "I can't" locks it.

**2) The deserving box.** "That's not for me." What I take by force, not what I ask for. The owner of this box works a lot and asks for little, then resents the ones who ask a lot and get it.

**3) The timing box.** "It's too early" or "the time has passed." The two are the same. The first postpones, the second apologises. Either way, life moves and the sentence stays still.

**4) The origin box.** "People like us don't do that." This sentence isn't about you — it's about a family, a country, a class, an environment. And it operates without anyone saying it out loud.

**5) The guilt box.** "I can't be happy while they're not." Its owner denies himself anything good because someone else doesn't have it. That isn't generosity; it's fear dressed in generosity's clothes.

**6) The appearance box.** "What will people say?" Not because people actually say anything — because he imagines them permanently, and in his head there's an audience sitting in judgment on every step.

**7) The safety box.** "I'm comfortable like this." And the comfort is real — but its price is that it became a condition. Anything new became a threat to comfort instead of a step.

And these types overlap. Some people live in the capability box and the origin box at once, and that's what makes their story look impossible.

## A story from life: Souad and the timing box

Souad is forty-three, and she has worked as an accountant in a small company for sixteen years. And every year, in January, she says one sentence: "Next year I'll start my own project."

And next year comes, and passes, and there's no project.

I asked her once: what happens in January?

She said: "I think about it."

I said: okay, and then?

She said: "Then I remember time is passing, so I push it to the year after."

And I asked her one question: "If this project isn't going to succeed, what's the worst thing that could happen?"

She was quiet for a long time, and then said something she hadn't expected: "That people find out accounting is the last thing I know how to do."

There the sentence appeared. The project wasn't postponed for money or time. It was postponed because if it failed, she'd have proof that she doesn't know how to do anything else. And postponing protects her from the proof.

The timing box isn't about time. The timing box is about **protecting an image.**

## A question before you go on

If you found yourself in one of the seven, don't say "that's just how I am." That sentence is itself the box, wearing the clothes of a diagnosis.

The box isn't a discovery about your personality. The box is a description of a condition, and conditions change.

## Signs you're inside a box (and the window is shut)

Not every sentence in your head is a box. There's ordinary talk, there are observations, there are opinions that change. A box has signs. Six of them — if you find three, start doubting.

**1) The sentence arrives before thought.** You don't think and then conclude. The sentence comes first, and the thinking comes after, justifying it.

**2) The sentence shuts down the next sentence.** The moment someone says "let's try," you find a ready answer in your head. Not a second of thinking. A ready answer.

**3) The sentence comforts you.** It doesn't upset you — no. It comforts you. The frustration comes later, when you find yourself standing in the same place.

**4) The sentence doesn't need evidence.** If someone asked you for proof, you'd get confused. Because it isn't built on evidence; it's built on repetition.

**5) The sentence gets said with a laugh.** Like Mostafa. The laugh is a sign the sentence is immunised against discussion.

**6) The sentence shows up in different situations in the same shape.** At work, in marriage, with money. Same sentence, different clothes.

## How do you tell a belief from a fact?

One question separates them: **did you try it, or did you imagine it?**

A fact holds up against experience. A belief either collapses or dodges.

And if you haven't tried, then you don't have a fact — you have an estimate. And an estimate isn't a fault; the fault is treating it as a final verdict.

There's another small sign: a fact isn't afraid of the question. A belief is. If you asked yourself "what's the evidence?" and what came out of you was irritation or a wish to change the subject — then you've found one.

## And self-estimate changes

There's something you need to know early, so you're not afraid of what's coming: **your estimate of yourself changes with every experience.**

This isn't motivational talk. It's an observation about people. A person who does something and sees a result keeps estimating himself higher at it. Not because he became a better human being, but because he has evidence.

And evidence accumulates. Every small experience lays a brick. The brick doesn't need to be big. The first time you say your opinion in a meeting, the first time you apologise, the first time you ask for a bigger number — each one adds evidence.

And the belief lives on the absence of evidence. The moment evidence increases, the sentence starts to lose ground. That's what will happen to you in the coming chapters, one step at a time.

## Exercise: the box of sentences

Take a sheet of paper. No — really. This sheet isn't a luxury; it's the difference between reading a book and doing something.

**Step one:** Write seven sentences you say about yourself often. Not the ones you wish you said. The ones you actually say. Start with the ones you say while laughing, like Mostafa.

**Step two:** Next to each sentence, write the first time you heard it (or the first time you remember hearing it). If you can't remember, write "can't remember" — that's an acceptable and important answer.

**Step three:** Ask about each sentence: what is this sentence keeping me from?

**Step four:** Next to each sentence, write: if this sentence turned out to be a lie, what would become possible?

Step four is the one that hurts. That's why it's the one that works.

## And what matters after you write them

Don't try to change them today. Or tomorrow. The goal of the exercise isn't change; the goal is **sight**.

Like someone finding something in his pocket after years — he doesn't use it that instant, but he knows now that he has it.

The sentences you write on this sheet are what we'll work on for the whole book. Keep the sheet. We'll come back to it in the second chapter, and the fifth, and at the end of the book.

## Something important before you go on

This book doesn't say everything is possible. That's a line sold by comfortable people.

This book says something narrower and truer: **the box you're in is not locked.** There's a door. The door isn't easy, and it doesn't open with talk, and nobody will open it for you. But it exists.

And if you're happy in your box, no problem. This book isn't only for the prisoner who hates his chains; it's for anyone who feels his life is narrower than he is.

## The question that annoys everyone: "How would I know?"

The moment you see your box, an uncomfortable question arrives: **how do I know this is a belief and not a fact?**

The answer isn't philosophy. It's three practical signs:

**1) A fact is confirmed from outside; a belief is confirmed from inside.** If the only thing proving it to you is your feeling or your own talk to yourself, then it's a belief. A fact has evidence you could show someone else.

**2) A fact doesn't get upset when you doubt it; a belief does.** Try telling yourself: "maybe this isn't true." If you feel tightness or a quick defence, then you've touched a belief, not information.

**3) A fact stays constant across situations; a belief changes depending on who's with you.** "I'm not a talker" shows up with your manager and disappears with your friends. That contradiction is proof it's a situational belief, not a fixed trait.

And there's a fourth question, the strongest: **"What would it cost me if this sentence were wrong?"** If the answer is "a lot," then this sentence is doing a job for you — it's protecting you from something. And a belief that protects doesn't leave until it finds another protection.

## A quick exercise

Take one minute: write the sentence you say about yourself more than any other. The one that comes out of you at work, at home, over coffee.

Then ask: **who was the first person to say it?** If you remembered a face, then it isn't your trait. It's something that was said to you at a particular time, and you're still hearing it in your own voice.

## The sentence of the week

"The sentence you say about yourself without thinking — that's the sentence that rules you."
