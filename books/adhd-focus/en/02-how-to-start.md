# Chapter Two: How to Start When You Can't Start

## The problem

The hardest moment in any task isn't the middle or the end — it's **the moment before you begin**.

You know the feeling: the task is in front of you, the time is free, nothing is stopping you… and nothing is making you start either. Your body sits still, your mind races, and everything nearby becomes fascinating. Even rearranging the sugar drawer feels urgent.

This is where most people go wrong: they wait for the **feeling of readiness**. Ready, focused, not irritated. But readiness doesn't arrive before the start — it's the *result* of the start. You cannot feel ready while standing still.

## The method: four start tricks

The goal isn't to finish the task. The goal is to survive **the first 120 seconds**, because after that your brain is on the track and the resistance drops.

### 1. The ridiculous first step (two minutes)

Choose a first step so small it's embarrassing: "open the file", "type a title", "get the notebook". Not "start working" — just "get the notebook".

The trick: your brain doesn't resist ridiculous things. And the moment you're seated with the file open, you've broken the biggest wall.

### 2. The anchor

Attach the start to something you already do, in the same order, without thinking:

> "After I put the coffee on the desk → I open the file."
> "After I close the room door → I start the timer."
> "After I finish a call → I write the first two lines before standing up."

The old habit becomes a hook for the new one, so you don't need to remember — the thing that happens before you is what starts you.

### 3. The transition ritual (three minutes)

Your brain needs a signal that work has begun. A small ritual separates life from work:

- One deep breath plus a glass of water on the desk
- Closing every tab except one
- Headphones plus one fixed playlist that plays only during work

That music is a strong tool precisely because it becomes a conditional signal: the moment it plays, your brain knows it's work time.

### 4. Remove the starting decision

The biggest energy drain is the decision "what do I start with?". So leave one sheet on the desk with **one line only**: the first step of the current task, written the night before.

In the morning you don't need to think — you need to execute a written line.

## The tool: the start card

Write a small card and keep it in front of you:

> **First 120 seconds:** [one ridiculous step]
> **Then immediately:** [the next step, so you don't stop]
> **Ritual:** [coffee / water / music / one tab]
> **If you resist:** [the smallest possible thing: just open the file and close it]

That last line matters most: **a plan for failure**. If you truly can't start, do the smallest thing (open the file, close it). The point is never to lose a whole day — a lost day becomes a lost week.

## Before / after

**Before:** "I have to start the report… I can't… what do I do?… I'll check my phone for a bit and come back." (An hour later, still on the phone.)

**After:** "Coffee on the desk (ritual). Open the file (ridiculous two minutes). Type the first heading (then immediately). Three paragraphs down and I didn't notice." Same person, same day — the start was just small enough.

## Chapter rule

> Don't wait to feel ready. Start with the ridiculous step; readiness arrives afterwards.

## Chapter exercise (7 minutes)

1. Pick one task you'll start tomorrow.
2. Write its "first 120 seconds" on a sheet and put it on the desk tonight.
3. Pick your ritual (one thing only).
4. Tomorrow morning, run the ritual plus the first 120 seconds, and record whether you did.

If you do this three days in a row, you no longer need to motivate yourself — you've built a **start**, and the start is the hardest part of all work.
