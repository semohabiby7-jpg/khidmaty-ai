# Chapter 5: Follow-Up Isn't Memory — Follow-Up Is a System

## The problem

Deals don't die at the moment of refusal. They die in **silence**.

The client asks, you answer, they say "I'll think about it" — and three weeks later you remember you were supposed to call, you send a message, and they're already halfway through someone else's project.

The problem isn't that you forgot. The problem is that there is **nowhere** for this to be written down. Your head is not a database and WhatsApp is not a table. If you're running three projects and fifty clients in the same month, relying on memory means the loudest client wins — not the one who is actually ready to buy.

There is a second, more dangerous problem: when follow-up lives in memory, you follow up with the clients you **like** or the ones that happen to stay in your head. The quiet deals get dropped — and quiet deals close more often than you'd think.

## The method: a four-column sheet, not a heavy CRM

Open any sheet — Google Sheets, even a notebook — with four columns only:

| Client | Stage | Last contact | Next step + date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ahmed | Viewed | Oct 4 | Send payment plan — Oct 6 |
| Sara | Wants alternatives | Oct 2 | Send 3 cheaper units — Oct 5 |
| Mostafa | Paused — back after the holiday | Sep 12 | Call — Oct 15 |

**Four columns only.** Every extra column lowers the chance you'll open the sheet at all. Fifteen columns and you'll abandon it in a week.

### The five stages

Keep stages few and blunt. More stages means more confusion:

1. **Inquiry** — just asked.
2. **Viewed** — seen it with their own eyes.
3. **Negotiating** — talking price or terms.
4. **Waiting** — waiting on someone else (bank, family, selling their old place).
5. **Closed or lost** — remove them from the sheet, don't leave them hanging.

A client should never sit in the same stage for more than two weeks without a reason. If they do, it means **you** don't know their real situation — and that alone justifies a call.

### The cadence: 2 – 7 – 21

You don't need to message every day. You need to message at the moments that matter:

| Timing | Goal | Message type |
|---|---|---|
| Hours after the viewing | Anchor the experience | Thanks + the feature they liked + one question |
| Day two | Add information | Payment plan, alternative unit, or an answer to their objection |
| Day seven | Decision | "Shall we continue, or look at something else?" — direct and respectful |
| Day twenty-one | Revive | Genuinely new information (price, unit, delivery) |
| Monthly after that | Stay present | A short, useful note — not an ad |

**The weekly rule:** Sunday morning, 30 minutes, open the sheet and touch every row whose "last contact" is older than seven days. Those are the most valuable 30 minutes of your week.

### The trap: automated follow-up feels automated

Send the same message to everyone and the client feels like a row in a list. Personalisation does not require long writing — **one line** lifted from their own words beats a polished paragraph. "You said the school was the deal-breaker…" — that single line makes the message personal.

## The ready prompt: an organised weekly follow-up

> You are a real-estate sales manager helping me organise my client follow-up. I'll give you my client list with stages and last-contact dates.
>
> **Deliver:**
> 1. Sort clients into three groups: (A) needs contact this week, (B) needs contact this month, (C) not worth pursuing — with the reason. Rule: any client past seven days in "Viewed" or "Negotiating" goes to (A).
> 2. For each client in (A), write a short WhatsApp message (three lines max) containing: a reference to something they said, new information or a practical step, and one question. "Still interested?" is banned.
> 3. For each client in (C), suggest a respectful closing message that leaves the door open without pressure.
> 4. Write my 30-minute Sunday plan: minute by minute, in order.
>
> **Client list:** [name — stage — last contact — short note on their objection]
>
> **Banned:** any information not in the list, any promise, and any long message.

### Before / after

**Before:** "Hi Ahmed, are you still interested in the unit?"

**After:** "Morning Ahmed. You said the school was the deal-breaker — I found out there's a bus stopping at the gate at 7:10, which was the exact point you raised. The unit is still available, and there's another with the same view and a lower down payment. Want me to send it?"

The difference isn't length. The difference is that the second one **proves you listened**. Clients reply to being heard, not to being asked.

## Mistakes that kill follow-up

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A 20-column sheet | You never open it again | Four columns only |
| Following up from memory | You chase who you like, not who's ready | A fixed weekly review |
| "Still interested?" | Closes the door on a reply | New information + a specific question |
| One copy-paste for everyone | They feel like a list entry | One line from their own words |
| Daily nagging | They block you | The 2–7–21 cadence |
| Leaving clients open forever | A sheet full of dead names | Close the file with a respectful note |

## Chapter exercise (15 minutes)

1. List every client in "Viewed" or "Negotiating".
2. Mark who hasn't been contacted in seven days.
3. Send each one a message with a line from their own words plus one new piece of information.
4. Record what happened: how many replied? That number is your fuel for next week.

## Chapter line

> A deal doesn't die when the client says no. It dies when nobody talks to them again.
