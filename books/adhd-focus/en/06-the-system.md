# Chapter Six: A System Outside Your Head

## The problem

You're asking your brain to do two jobs at once: **remember** and **think**. Those two fight each other.

Everything you carry in your head — "I need to do this", "don't forget that", "when is that appointment?" — takes up space. Not much per item, but twelve open loops mean your brain is permanently in storage mode, with no room left to work.

And you see the result daily: you sit down to work, suddenly remember something else, get up to do it, and lose where you were. That isn't distraction — it's a brain trying to rescue what it's afraid of losing.

**The fix isn't being smarter or faster.** It's handing the load to someone else.

## The core idea: your brain is a processor, not a warehouse

> **If it isn't written down, it doesn't exist.**

Not because you forget — you do forget, we all do. But the bigger problem is that an unwritten item keeps circling, consuming attention, and keeping you in a low-level "unsettled" state all day.

The moment you write it down, something happens: your brain lets go. Not because it stopped being responsible — because it's now reassured it's in a safe place.

## Tool 1: one inbox

For most people the problem isn't that they don't write things down — it's that they write in **five places**: a sheet on the desk, a phone note, a WhatsApp message to themselves, a voice memo, and their head. Result: no single place you can ask "what do I have?"

**Rule: one place only.** Anything new that comes to mind goes there. One place, known, open in a second.

The format doesn't matter. A notes app, a small notebook, one file. What matters is that it's **one**, and reachable in under ten seconds.

## Tool 2: the ten-minute review

A single inbox without review is a graveyard. You need a fixed, daily, short appointment: **ten minutes at the same time every day.**

Three questions per line:
1. **When does this happen?** → if it has a day and time, it goes to the calendar.
2. **It happens, but not now?** → it stays on the list.
3. **It won't happen?** → it goes. Really. Not "I'll look later".

Step three is the most important one, and the one nobody does. A list with forty items is the same problem you were escaping by writing.

## Tool 3: the list of three

Any long list becomes psychological background noise. Your eye sees it, your brain feels the weight, and you go do something else.

So: **three per day.**
1. **One important** — if only this gets done, the day is a success.
2. **One quick** — done in fifteen minutes, removes a nagging weight.
3. **One backup** — if the day collapses, this rescues it.

The rest lives on the long list, out of sight. The long list exists to **reassure you**, not to work from.

## Tool 4: visible time

Abstract time is the enemy. "I'll finish in half an hour" means nothing to your brain — nothing moves, nothing measures.

**Make time visible.** A digital timer, an hourglass, an app — anything that moves in front of you. When you see time moving, something in your brain responds.

There's a second benefit: a timer gives you an **end**. A task with no end is scarier than a task with one. "Twenty-five minutes only" is far easier than "finish it".

## Tool 5: the transition card

When you leave a task and come back two hours later, the first ten minutes go to "what was I doing?" — and the mental state is gone.

The **transition card** fixes that. Before stepping away, write three lines:
1. **Where I stopped**, exactly.
2. **The next step** in one line.
3. **An idea I don't want to lose** (if any).

When you return, you don't start from zero — you read three lines and continue. One minute of writing saves ten.

## Tool 6: the simple system always wins

This is where most energy is wasted: **hunting for the perfect system.**

You try an app, decide after a week it's wrong, try another, build a colour-coded setup, read about a third… and a month later you're not using any system, but you have expertise in systems.

> **The simplest system you actually use beats the best system you don't.**

If capturing something takes more than three steps, you'll quit. Shrink it until it's easier than leaving it in your head.

## When the tool becomes the problem

One clear sign: **you're organising more than you're working.**
- You reorganise your apps every week.
- You keep three task lists and copy between them.
- You build a new system every time the old one fails.

The right system takes weight off your brain, not your time. If you feel you're managing the system more than working with it, **go back to the simplest version** and stay there.

## Before / after

**Before:** fifteen things in his head, sits down to work, remembers another one and gets up, ends the day exhausted with nothing finished.

**After:** one page holding everything, three items in sight, a timer running, a ten-minute review at night. Same person — but his mind is free for work instead of busy with storage.

## Chapter rule

> Your brain is not a warehouse. Put the load outside and let it do what it's good at.

## Chapter exercise (15 minutes)

1. **Pick one place** — notebook or app; don't overthink the choice.
2. **Move everything in your head** there — even the small and the strange. Ten minutes of writing, done.
3. **Set a daily alarm** for ten minutes at the same time, for review.
4. **Write tomorrow's list of three**: one important, one quick, one backup.

If you do this properly, the first thing you'll notice isn't that you're organised — it's that you're **calmer**.
