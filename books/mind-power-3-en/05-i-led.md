# Chapter Five: I Led

There's a difference between having power... and using it. You understood in the previous parts that you have power — you reclaimed it, directed it, arrived, evaluated, and benefited. All excellent. All necessary. But there's one final question before we reach people: "What am I leading with it?"

Because power without leadership... is like a car with a powerful engine left in the middle of a field. It's running. But it's not going anywhere. Leadership is what makes power move in a direction. Without it — power works, but doesn't arrive.

And we'll begin as we always begin... with a story.

## The Story of Heba and the Car That Drives Itself

Heba is thirty-two. A good graphic designer. Self-taught from YouTube, freelanced for five years, has a respectable portfolio, clients who return. On paper — Heba arrived. And that's not just talk — people see it in her work.

Heba also did everything in this book: determined her destination (to become a professional designer), arrived (she did), evaluated her work (knew it was good), believed (trusted she deserved it), and benefited (taught people, streamlined her work, raised her rates).

And after all that... Heba is still sitting in the back seat.

Tonight captures it perfectly. Heba is sitting with her friend Sara, telling her about a major client who offered a month-long contract — big work, good money, a real opportunity. Sara said: "Great, Heba, accept!"

Heba said: "Maybe... but I want to ask Amr first. He's my friend and understands contracts."

Amr said: "Take your time, don't accept right away."

Heba asked her mother. Her mother said: "But you're not married, work won't let you marry."

Heba asked her brother. Her brother said: "If this job pressures you at home, don't accept."

Heba... didn't accept. The client left. And she told herself: "Well, it's better to wait for a better opportunity."

But that opportunity didn't come. And Heba stayed as she was.

A week after the client left, Heba saw Dina. Dina is three years younger. Less skilled. But Dina opened a small studio. Dina is the one who decides. Dina is the one who drives.

Heba asked herself a strange question: "Dina has half of what I have. But Dina drives. And I... sit in the back."

And then she understood. The understanding was shocking and simple at the same: she has all the power — but the power isn't driving. Because her hands aren't on the steering wheel. Someone else is driving. And where is she going? Wherever the road takes her.

## The Steering Wheel

The steering wheel — you know it. Every car has one. And the one holding the wheel is the one who turns, and the one who turns decides the direction, and the one who decides the direction... is the one who arrives.

But here's something important: the car moves whether you're driving or someone else is. The engine runs. The tires spin. The car moves. The difference is only — where it's going. If you're driving, it goes where you want. If someone else is driving, it goes where they want. And the result? In both cases the car arrived. But in one case, it arrived at your destination. In the other — at someone else's.

And that's the condition of many of us: we have power. We have skill. We have experience. But our hands aren't on the wheel. We leave the steering to: "what people say," "what circumstances say," "what family says," "what fear says." And the car moves — but in a direction that isn't ours.

This whole chapter says one thing: your hands on the wheel. Not because you're smarter than the people you ask. Because this car is yours. And where it goes — that's your decision, not someone's opinion.

## The Idea in Points

### First: Leadership isn't a decision... it's a stance

Some people wait until they're "ready" to lead. And they'll never be ready. Because leadership isn't a skill you learn completely and then begin. Leadership is a stance — the decision that you're the one who decides. From this moment. Not when you become an expert. Not when you finish all the courses. From the moment your hands are on the wheel — you're driving. Even if you're still learning.

Heba didn't lack information. She lacked a stance. The stance to say: "I decide." Not "I want an opinion" — opinions are taken with caution. But the decision is yours. Because you're the one who'll bear the consequence. And anyone else who says "do this" — won't bear it with you. They say and walk away. And you're the one who'll live after.

### Second: There's a difference between "listening" and "obeying"

The leader isn't deaf. The one who leads their life listens — hears advice, hears opinions, hears experiences. But the difference between them and the one sitting in the back: they listen and decide. Not listen and obey. Obedience to advice means you've handed the wheel to the advisor. And listening means you take the information and return your hands to the wheel.

Heba was asking people not to listen — to obey. Every time someone gave an opinion, she took it as a decision. And the car turned in the direction the last person told her to go. Not in the direction she wanted.

The true leader says: "Thank you for your opinion. I'll think about it. And I'll decide." The last sentence is what makes the difference. "I'll decide" — not "I'll bless your opinion."

### Third: Leadership starts from the smallest decision

Some people wait for "the big decision" to take it and say "I led." But leadership doesn't only affect big decisions — it affects small ones. What you'll eat for breakfast. When you'll sleep. Whether you'll reply to this message. Whether you'll work now or in an hour.

These small decisions are the exercises. Every small decision you make yourself — your hands grow stronger on the wheel. And every time you leave it to someone else — your hands grow weaker. And then one day the big decision comes... and you find your hands can't hold. Because you didn't practice.

Start small. Trivial decisions. "I decide what I eat for breakfast." "I decide what I wear." "I decide whether to reply now or later." Each small decision = a leadership exercise. After a few weeks — you'll find your hands strong on the wheel. And big decisions became easier. Because your hands are used to it.

### Fourth: Leadership isn't ignoring people — it's respecting them more

There's a fear of leadership: "If I decide, people will be upset." Wrong idea. The true leader doesn't ignore people — they respect them properly. True respect isn't doing everything people say. True respect is saying: "I heard you. And I decide. And if my decision is wrong, I'll carry it." People respect the one who bears responsibility for their decision. They don't respect the one who distributes their decision to others and then blames them.

Heba, when she asked five people, wasn't bearing responsibility. She was distributing it. And when the client left, she blamed "circumstances" and "Amr's opinion" and "her mother's advice." She didn't blame herself. Because she wasn't the one who decided. And that's exactly the difference: the leader bears. The passenger complains.

## Chapter Exercise: The "I Led" Page

**What you need:** Paper and pen, and ten minutes.

1. **At the top, write:** "I decide." Write five decisions in your life right now — small or large — that you've left to someone else. Not necessarily that they "took" them — but that you agreed without choosing. Five.

2. **Below each decision, write:** "If I were the one deciding... what would I do?" Honestly. Not "what's right" — "what do you want." If no one was watching. If no one would be upset. If it were just you and your hands on the wheel.

3. **Ask yourself:** "Who am I giving the wheel to?" For each of the five: who's driving? Your mother? Your friend? Fear? Circumstances? Write the name.

4. **Choose one decision from the five... and take it.** Not five. One. One only. A small decision. Take it this week. Don't negotiate. Don't ask. You decide. And if it's wrong — you carry it. And that's exactly leadership.

## Weekly Sentence

**"The car moves whether you're driving or not. The difference is: where you arrive."**

Write it and keep it in front of you. All week, whenever you find yourself asking someone "what should I do?" before a decision — ask: "If I didn't ask, what would I do?" And that answer... is yours. Take it.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

You determined. You arrived. You evaluated and believed. You benefited. You led. Five rungs on the ladder. And you have power, benefit, and leadership. But all this... for whom? Just you? And the final question — the question this whole book leads to — is: "Who did I benefit?" How what you learned, did, and led... becomes good for someone else. That's the peak of the ladder. And it's the next chapter.
