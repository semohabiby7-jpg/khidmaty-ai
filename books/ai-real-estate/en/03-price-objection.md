# Chapter 3: "The Price Is Too High" — Answering It Without Cutting a Dollar

## The problem

The client says: "The price is too high."

And you answer immediately with one of three things: a discount, a slogan like "prices are going up", or a comparison to another unit that doesn't actually exist.

All three are wrong, for the same reason: **the sentence they said is not the real objection.**

"Too expensive" is what people say when they have nothing else to say. In reality it is one of three completely different things, and each one needs a different answer. When you respond with a discount, you treat one type and lose money on all three.

There is a second thing worth knowing. In real estate, "too expensive" is often pointing at something else entirely: "I'm not sure about the neighbourhood", "I don't know if the bank will approve me", "my wife isn't convinced". "Expensive" is the easiest thing to say when you can't name the real worry — or when you don't yet trust the agent. That is why the first skill here is not the reply. It is **the question**.

## The method: split "expensive" into three types

| Objection type | What the client says | The question you ask | What you send |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expensive vs. the market | "There's a cheaper unit in the same community" | "What's the cheapest unit you saw with the same specs, and how big was it?" | Price per square foot — not the total |
| Expensive vs. their budget | "I have 3.5 and I can't go higher" | "What down payment are you comfortable with, and what monthly payment?" | A payment plan or financing — not a discount |
| Expensive vs. the value | "I don't think it's worth it" | "What are your top three must-haves?" | Re-frame the value around *their* priorities |

**The golden rule:** never cut the price before you know the type. A discount answers only the first type, and it costs you on all three. Worse, an instant discount tells the client your first price wasn't honest — and from then on they haggle over everything.

### How to ask without cornering them

"how much money do you have?" closes the conversation. Three phrasings open it:

1. **The comparison phrasing:** "You've probably seen other units in the area — which one came closest to what you liked?" (about units, not about their wallet)
2. **The range phrasing:** "Most of my clients in this bracket put down between [x] and [y] — are you near that?" (a range, not an interrogation)
3. **The priority phrasing:** "Let's park the price for a minute — what has to be in the home?" (about desire, and desire is the first step toward price)

The reply changes with their answer. What matters is that you never ask in a way that makes them defend themselves.

### Why price per square foot beats every other answer

The client compares the **total**. You should compare the **rate**.

| Unit | Size | Price | Price/ft | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yours | 1,400 | 4.2M | 3,000 | Move-in ready |
| The one they saw | 1,600 | 3.9M | 2,437 | Shell and core |
| Difference | −200 | +300k | +563/ft | + finishing cost |

In this example the other unit is "cheaper" by 300k in total, yet it is more expensive once you add finishing and the extra space they don't need. The number that carries the truth is price per foot, not the number on the sign.

**One caution:** use this comparison only with real, verified numbers. Invent a competitor's figure and you'll be caught on the first call and lose the client for good. And compare like with like: same area, roughly same floor, same condition.

## The four-step reply frame

Every price objection runs through this order, and the order is what prevents the discount:

1. **Acknowledge.** "You're right, it's a big number." — without agreeing it's expensive, and without denying how it feels. Denial makes them push harder.
2. **Ask the sorting question.** Just one, from the three above.
3. **Re-frame.** Move from total price to price per foot, from the amount to the payment plan, or from the specs to their priorities.
4. **Ask for a small step.** "Want me to send it?" / "Shall we see it on Saturday?" — objections dissolve in motion, not in argument.

### Step four is where most agents lose the deal

The agent delivers excellent information… and then goes quiet. The client says "okay, I'll think about it" and the deal cools. A complete reply ends with a question that moves one small thing, not with full persuasion.

## The ready prompt: price-objection analysis

> You are an experienced real-estate sales advisor in the Egyptian market, and your job is to help me answer a price objection without discounting.
>
> **Unit data:** [size, price, price per sq ft, down payment, monthly, finishing, two nearest amenities, area market price if available]
> **Client data:** [what I know, stated budget, priorities, cash or financing, their objection word for word]
>
> **Deliver:**
> 1. Classify the objection: market / budget / value. Explain in two lines, and if the data is insufficient tell me which question is missing.
> 2. Write three phrasings of the sorting question, each in a different style (comparison, range, priority).
> 3. Write three complete replies, each following the four steps: acknowledge, ask, re-frame, small step. About 60 words each, in plain conversational English.
> 4. Build a price-per-sq-ft comparison against a competing unit — only if the numbers are in the data; otherwise write "need market figure".
> 5. Name the strongest argument **for them**, not for me, and one argument I must avoid.
>
> **Banned:** any number not in the data, any promise of appreciation or guaranteed return, and phrases like "once in a lifetime" or "prices are going up".

### Before / after

**Before:** "There's a small discount if you're ready to close today."

That reply tells the client three things: your price was negotiable all along, you are in a hurry, and the unit isn't strong enough to sell at its own price.

**After:** "You're right, it's a big number. Let's put it per square foot: the unit you saw is about 2,440 per foot and larger, but it's shell and core and needs at least [amount] in finishing. This one is 3,000 per foot and move-in ready tomorrow. And if the down payment is the real concern, there's a three-installment plan on it. Want me to send it to you?"

The second doesn't claim the unit is cheap. It hands the client the numbers to decide with — and lets them make the decision. That is what moves a deal.

## Ready replies for each type

### 1. Expensive vs. the market

> "That makes sense. Quick question: what's the cheapest unit you saw in the same community, and how big was it? … The gap between them is [amount], and the gap in finishing is [amount]. I'm not telling you mine is cheaper — I'm telling you the honest comparison is price per foot. Want me to send you a table with both?"

### 2. Expensive vs. their budget

> "That's a clear number and I respect it — I don't want you taking on a commitment you're not comfortable with. If the down payment is the obstacle, we have [plan]. If it's the monthly, we have [longer term]. What number, if you heard it, would make you say 'that I can do'?"

### 3. Expensive vs. the value

> "Agreed, the number deserves a conversation. But let me understand first: what are the three things that have to be in the home you live in? … Here's the honest answer: of those three, this unit gives you [x and y], but it won't give you [z]. If that matters to you, then honestly it isn't worth the money to you — and in that case I have another unit that fits you better."

That last sentence is the strongest in the chapter. Telling a client a unit isn't right for them builds the trust that makes everything else you say believable. Selling isn't closing everyone on everything.

## Mistakes that kill the deal

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Instant discount | They haggle on everything, and your price credibility breaks | Ask first |
| "Prices go up next week" | Said every week, believed by nobody | A verified fact, or silence |
| Comparing to a unit you're unsure about | You get caught on the first call | Real numbers only |
| "You don't understand the market" | They shut down completely | "Let me walk you through the math" |
| A long argument | Every minute of arguing lowers the odds of closing | Acknowledge, re-frame, move |
| Promising a guaranteed rental yield | Real legal exposure | A sourced, clearly estimated range |

## Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Take the last three clients who said "too expensive".
2. Classify each: market / budget / value.
3. Write the question you should have asked instead of discounting.
4. Count how many times you cut the price without needing to — that number is the cost of the missing question.

## Chapter line

> The client is not complaining about the price. The client is complaining that they can't yet see why that number is worth it. Your job is to clarify, not to cave.
