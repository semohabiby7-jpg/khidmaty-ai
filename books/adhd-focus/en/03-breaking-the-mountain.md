# Chapter Three: Breaking the Mountain

## The problem

Some tasks sit in front of you like a mountain. Not technically hard — just **big**. "Prepare the project file", "write the report", "sort my accounts", "make the plan".

When a task is that big, your brain doesn't see a task — it sees a **mass**. And a mass has no beginning, so there's nothing to start from. That's why you can spend an hour thinking about a task and not one second working.

And something else happens: every time you think about it, it grows. Because when your brain imagines a vague task, it fills the blanks with the hardest possible version. A vague task gets bigger with every thought.

The fix isn't more courage. It's **making the task small enough to touch**.

## The core idea: it's not a mountain, it's accumulated gravel

Any big task = a project. A project = stages. A stage = steps. A step = something you can do in 10–15 minutes **while tired**.

The problem is you're dealing with level one only (the project), while you actually work at level three (the step). That's why you feel like you're facing something with no entrance.

**Boundary rule:** if you can't write the first step in one line, the fault isn't your willpower — the task is still **undefined**.

## The method: the mountain map (three columns)

Take a sheet and draw three columns:

| Mountain | Rocks | Gravel |
|---|---|---|
| the big task | the stages | the steps (10–15 min) |

**Example — "prepare the project file":**

- **Rocks:** 1) gather the numbers · 2) write the description · 3) review and send
- **Gravel (under rock one):** open the project folder · pull this month's invoice · put the numbers in a table · check the numbers against the statement

See the difference: "prepare the project file" doesn't move. "Open the project folder" moves in 30 seconds.

## The gravel test: three conditions

A step isn't a step unless it passes all three:

1. **Physical:** you can see it, touch it, or write it — not "think about it" or "organise my thoughts".
2. **Photographable:** if someone photographed you doing it, the picture would show clear work.
3. **Doable while tired:** if it needs high energy, it's a stage not a step — break it down further.

Any step that fails these three is a **heading**, not a step.

## The "three steps are enough" rule

Here's an important trap: breaking things down becomes its own procrastination. You keep breaking down and breaking down and build a beautiful plan… and never start. Planning becomes a polite form of escape.

So: **break down three steps only, and start the first one immediately.** Full decomposition isn't required before starting — new steps appear while you work, not while you think.

## How do you know the task is broken down properly?

One sign: **your resistance drops**. If it still feels heavy, the gravel is still too big. Keep breaking it down.
If you feel "this is simple, I can do it now" — you're done decomposing, and it's work time, not planning time.

## The tool: one step on the desk

As in the previous chapter, the desk holds **one line only** — the first piece of gravel. Not the whole map, not the long list. A long list invites you to read it and close it.

## The elephant and the slices

An old saying goes that you eat an elephant bite by bite. The problem is everyone repeats it and nobody applies it, because **the wrong slice is too big**.

The right elephant slice finishes before your brain gets bored. 10–15 minutes. Anything more turns a slice back into a mountain.

## Before / after

**Before:** "I have the monthly report to do… it'll take days… I don't know where to start." (Two hours later: still thinking, and did two completely unrelated things.)

**After:** "First rock: the numbers. First gravel: open this month's folder. Second gravel: pull the first branch invoice." Ten minutes, and it's moving.

## Chapter rule

> A task not written as a step is a task that doesn't exist yet. Shrink it until you can touch it.

## Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Take the biggest task you've been postponing.
2. Write it in the "mountain" column. Under it, three "rocks" only.
3. Under the first rock, write four pieces of "gravel" that pass all three conditions (physical · photographable · doable while tired).
4. Put the first piece of gravel alone on a sheet on the desk.
5. Do it **right now** — before you close the notebook.

Do this exercise on three big tasks and you'll discover that what was stopping you wasn't the size of the work — it was the size of the **ambiguity**.
