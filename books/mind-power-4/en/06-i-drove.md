# Chapter Six: I Drove

There's a difference between someone who drives and someone who's driven. Not in the car. In life. The one who drives knows the road, chooses the speed, brakes when needed, and arrives where he wants. The one who's driven... arrives where the driver takes him. And if he doesn't know who the driver is... he doesn't know where he's arrived.

And we'll start — as always — with a story.

## Dalia, Who Drove Her Own Life

Dalia is thirty-nine. HR director at a large company. Knows her job, respected, and makes decisions with confidence. At work. At home, a completely different story.

Dalia has been married for fifteen years. Her husband is a good man — not an angel, good. But there's something in those fifteen years that kept repeating: every time a big decision came up — finances, the kids' education, renovating the house, even planning a vacation — Dalia would delay. Not because she couldn't decide. Because she waited. Waited for her husband to decide. Waited for circumstances to determine. Waited for the "right time." And the result: decisions delayed for months. And every delay built pressure. And every pressure built a fight. And every fight started and ended with the same sentence: "Why didn't we do this sooner?"

One day, Dalia sat with herself. Not to change. To look. She asked the question the whole book revolves around: "Who's driving?" And the answer appeared — clear, painful, but true: at work, Dalia drives. At home, Dalia is driven. Not her husband driving her. Fear. The fear of deciding and being wrong. The fear of bearing the responsibility of a decision. The fear of leading... and being held accountable.

Dalia decided something simple. Not to change her life. Something simpler: one decision. One decision she'd make herself — at home. A small decision: renovating the living room. She chose the color. She asked about the price. She called the carpenter. She made the decision. Didn't wait. Didn't wait. Didn't delay.

And what happened next was strange. Not that her husband was upset. Not that she made a mistake. What happened: she felt something she hadn't felt in years. She felt she was in the seat. Not because the decision was big — it was small. But because she was the one who made it. She was the one who led it. She was the one who saw its result.

And after the small decision... a bigger one. And after the bigger one... bigger. And every decision widened the distance between her and the fear. And every distance brought something new: confidence. Confidence isn't not being afraid. Confidence is being afraid... and driving anyway.

## Your Seat

In the first chapter, we said there's a driver's seat. And that the one sitting in it changes — sometimes you, sometimes emotion. And that the problem is: you don't feel the switch.

After six chapters — after you learned you were driven, and named your feelings, and stopped a second, and unpacked your decision, and chose with awareness — now you have something you didn't have at the beginning: you know who's in the seat. And that's the difference.

Because the problem isn't that emotion sits in the seat. The problem is that it sits and you don't know. And when you know... you can. You can do something you can't do when you don't know: choose when you drive... and when you let emotion ride with you.

Because emotion won't move to the back seat on its own. Emotion always wants the seat. That's its nature — not its fault. Emotion is old, fast, strong. And if you let it, it'll drive. And if you don't let it... it rides. And if it rides... you tell it when to brake and when to move and when to stop. And you're the one who decides.

## The Idea in Points

### First: leadership isn't controlling emotions... leadership is choosing who drives

There's a dangerous illusion: that leadership means you don't feel. That you're cold. That you control all your emotions. That's not true. And it's dangerous. Because if you try to control your emotions by force... you'll explode.

Leadership means: you feel — and you know. You choose who drives. Sometimes emotion. Sometimes reason. Sometimes value. And you're the one choosing who.

Situations with real danger — emotion drives. If your child runs toward the street, you don't need to think. Emotion moves. It saves. And that's right.

Situations with a big decision — value drives. If you're deciding to change your job, don't follow fear. Or excitement. Follow what you want. Value.

Situations with information — reason drives. If you're making a financial decision, emotion doesn't know. Reason calculates. Let it drive.

Leadership: knowing what the situation needs... and choosing. And the more you learn, the faster you get at choosing. And the more you learn, the more you become you.

### Second: leadership starts with a small decision

Dalia didn't change her life in a day. She started with a small decision: a room color. That small decision did something: it proved to her that she could. And every time she proved it to herself... she could more. And every time she could more... a bigger decision. And every bigger decision... more distance between her and the fear.

You don't have to start with a big decision. Start small. A decision you've been delaying for weeks. A message you haven't sent. A call you haven't made. Something small. Take it. And feel the feeling that comes after: that you were the one who drove.

This isn't arrogance. This is building. Building starts with a piece. And the piece is a small decision. And the small decision becomes a big foundation.

### Third: the real driver drives with emotion and reason together

The conscious driver isn't the one who cancels emotion. He's the one who drives with emotion and value together. Emotion says: "There's danger." Value says: "This is what I want." Reason calculates the road. And the driver takes all three... and chooses.

That's real leadership: not that you become a machine. That you become a human — who feels, thinks, and walks toward what he believes in. And that human — who combines emotion and reason and value — is you. Not emotion becoming someone. Not reason becoming someone. You — the one above all of them.

### Fourth: the passenger who doesn't know... stays a passenger forever

The one who doesn't know he's a passenger... stays a passenger forever. Because he doesn't try to drive. Doesn't try to choose. Doesn't try to stop. Because he doesn't know there's a switch happening.

But the one who knows — even once — changes. Not that he became a driver in a moment. That he became someone who knows the difference. And this knowing — if it comes with a stop and an unpacking and a decision — turns into a nature. And the nature turns into a life. And the life turns into leadership.

## Chapter Exercise: You're the One Driving

The last exercise. And the simplest. Needs paper and pen. And six days.

Every day, take one small decision — a decision you've been delaying. Not a big one. A small one. A message you haven't sent. A call you haven't made. Something in the house that needs doing. Something at work that needs doing.

**Take it yourself. Don't ask anyone. Don't wait. Take it.**

And then, ask yourself:

1. **"Before I took it... who was in the seat?"** (Fear? Emotion? Delay?)
2. **"After I took it... who's in the seat now?"** (You.)
3. **"What am I feeling?"** (Write the feeling — whether it's fear, relief, or excitement.)

Six days. Six small decisions. On the seventh day, look at the paper. You'll find something: the seat is yours. And the one sitting in it... you choose. And every time you choose... you become more.

## Weekly Sentence

**I drive. Emotion is with me... not ahead of me.**

Live with this for seven days. Every time you take a decision, repeat the sentence. Emotion won't disappear. Emotion will stay. But it won't drive. The one who drives... is you.
