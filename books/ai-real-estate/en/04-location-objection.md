# Chapter 4: "It's Far" and "I Don't Feel Comfortable Here" — The Objection That Isn't About Price

## The problem

After money, two objections kill more deals than anything else — and they usually meet the same wrong answer:

**"It's not far at all."**

That sentence loses the client, because it tells them their feeling is wrong. Distance is not a number on a map — distance is **time**, and comfort is a **feeling**. Neither is fixed by arguing. They are fixed by measuring and by experiencing.

## The method, part one: distance is time, not kilometres

The client says "far" while calculating in kilometres. Your job is to turn it into minutes, tied to **their** day, not someone else's.

### The four-route map

Ask about four things only, and write the number beside each:

| Route | Who uses it | Peak time | Off-peak time | Acceptable to them? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Work | Every day | | | |
| School | Every morning | | | |
| Family / friends | Weekly | | | |
| Nearest mall / hospital | When needed | | | |

**The rule:** if the peak-time total is above 90 minutes a day, the client is right — and your persuasion will collapse a week after they move in. If it is under 60, the number itself opens the conversation. Not your words.

**One caution:** never measure on an empty road. Measure at peak time, and ideally measure with them in the car. Let them drive the route once, because **experience beats argument every time**.

### The five-minute rule

Between two units, the real difference is often 5–10 minutes. If the further unit saves 500k, the daily difference might be 10 minutes each way — 20 minutes a day, about two hours a week. Convert both sides into one currency the client can compare. That is what ends the argument.

## The method, part two: comfort, split into three things

"I don't feel comfortable in this area" is not one objection. It is three, and each needs a different answer:

| Type | The tell in their words | The right response |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety** | "the street is dark", "there's no security", "strangers around" | A night visit + gate/security + neighbours |
| **Belonging** | "it's not my crowd", "not our kind of area" | Neighbours like them + community + activity |
| **Status / reputation** | "nobody knows it", "what will people say", "no resale market" | Resale value + upcoming projects + known names |

Ask one question only: **"When you say you're not comfortable, what's the first thing that comes to mind?"** The answer places you on one of the three.

### The fix is not persuasion, it is experience

Comfort is not built in a phone call. It is built in **a second visit at a different time**. If they saw it on a normal morning and felt uneasy, take them at sunset or after dark. If the worry is the people, let them sit for half an hour in a café or a park and just watch. What they see with their own eyes beats anything you say.

## The ready prompt: location-objection analysis

> You are a real-estate advisor who knows how to break down location objections. A client said: "[the unit is far]" or "[the area doesn't feel right]".
>
> **Unit data:** [address, nearest main road, minutes to: their work, school, family, mall/hospital — peak and off-peak]
> **Client data:** [workplace, kids' schools, how much proximity to family matters, daily routine]
>
> **Deliver:**
> 1. Identify the objection type: distance, safety, belonging, or reputation. Explain in two lines.
> 2. If distance: build the four-route table in minutes, calculate the daily and weekly time difference against the price difference, and convert it into one currency (hours per month, money per year). If numbers are missing, say "needs measurement".
> 3. If comfort: write three questions that open the topic without cornering them, and three practical experience steps (a visit at a specific time, who they should meet, what they should see).
> 4. Write a complete five-line reply: acknowledge, unpack, a number or an experience, a small step.
>
> **Banned:** denying their feeling, the phrase "it's not far", any unverified promise about the area's future or development, and guessed numbers.

### Before / after

**Before:** "It's really not far — it's straight down the main road, you'll be there in no time."

**After:** "Fair enough, the feeling of distance is real. Let's measure it against your day: how long is work from here at peak time? … And the school? … That's [n] minutes a day in total. The unit you saw before was [n], so the difference is [n] minutes a day, against [amount] in price for the same finishing. Want me to drive the route with you once after work and see the real number?"

The second doesn't deny anything and doesn't sell a fantasy. It turns an argument into a measurement — and measurement is what persuades.

## Mistakes that kill the location objection

| Mistake | Why it fails | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "It's not far" | Tells the client their feeling is wrong | "That feeling makes sense — let's measure it" |
| Numbers in kilometres | No human meaning | Minutes at peak time |
| "The area will develop" with no evidence | A promise that costs you your reputation in two years | A documented project, or nothing |
| Arguing about safety | Increases the anxiety | Night visit + security + neighbours |
| Ignoring comfort | You keep talking price while they see nothing | Ask: "what's the first thing that comes to mind?" |
| Persuading without experience | Words are forgotten, experience stays | A second visit at a different time |

## Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Take the last client who said "far" — write the four routes with real minute counts.
2. Take the last client who said "not comfortable" — label the type: safety / belonging / status.
3. Write the experience step you should have offered each of them.
4. Notice how many deals closed after a second visit — that number is your argument next time.

## Chapter line

> Distance is measured in minutes, and comfort is measured in experience. You don't need to convince the client their feeling is wrong — you need to hand them a number and an experience to judge with.
