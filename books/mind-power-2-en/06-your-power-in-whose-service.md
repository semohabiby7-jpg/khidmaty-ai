# Chapter Six: Your Power, in Service of Whom?

Let's gather all the threads of the book in one hand: you knew where you're going (destination), you closed a door (decision), you watered every day (patience), you returned at the smallest degree after every storm, and you arranged the people around you.

Which means you are now — by the technical measure — **a strong person.**

But let me ask you a question no one dares ask until they collide with it: strong... for whom?

Because there comes a moment in everyone's life that has no name in the books — a moment that reaches you alone. A moment where you feel that everything you've built... is good, and ordered, and walking — but something is missing no matter how much you add. And this chapter is about exactly that thing: the thing that isn't bought... **meaning.**

We'll begin the way we always begin... with a story.

## Mostafa's Story, and the Broken Ceiling

Mostafa is in his thirties. He owns a phone repair workshop in a popular neighbourhood. This workshop isn't a passing dream — it's the result of fourteen years of work: seven years working for someone else, three years of loan and installments, the gap years we talked about... and the year of the storm when the world hit and he learned to return at the smallest degree. The workshop is called «Mostafa Service» — by his name. Just as he dreamed, exactly, when he was sixteen.

Mostafa arrived. The income became good, the name became known in the neighbourhood, and the goal that was drawn on a destination page years ago became a reality that had been stable for three years.

And after he arrived... the strange thing happened.

One Friday, sitting at the café alone, he felt something that has no name in his dictionary: the workshop is running, the money is running... and every day became a repeated copy of the one before it: opening, repair, closing, money counted in the notebook. The power he was gathering for a goal... found its goal... and walked off. **The ceiling — he reached it... and the ceiling is a tight place.**

The story closed at Madame Zeinab.

A woman in her sixties, living in the building whose ground floor holds Mostafa's workshop. She came into the shop one afternoon with her phone in her hand as if it were something broken from its heart: «My son is in Saudi Arabia... and the phone crashed. All his messages are inside it. Can it be fixed, or should I get a new one?»

Mostafa looked at the phone: a simple fault, its price at any other shop: a hundred and fifty pounds. And he looked at her face as she said «my son's messages». He fixed it in half an hour, and took the money... and the strange thing happened: he felt that this half-hour had in it a meaning of a different kind. Not money — something cheaper and more precious: **his power turning for someone.**

From the Friday after, Mostafa set a rule in the workshop, written by his hand on a cardboard above the counter: «Friday: first two hours — repair at comfort price for pension women and students.» The word spread in the neighbourhood faster than any paid ad.

A year later: the workshop expanded and he had a craftsman working with him, and the workshop became a station not a place — people connecting to each other, Madame Zeinab telling her story to every woman in the neighbourhood salon, and the supplier started selling to him with a light heart because «this Mostafa is a respectable man.» The whole neighbourhood market describes what happened in one word: «his luck was good.»

But you know and I know that luck had no work here. What happened has another name entirely:

Before Madame Zeinab... Mostafa was **successful.** After her he became **meaningful.** And the difference between those two words is this entire chapter.

## Why Power Never Completes on Its Own

### First: The Broken Ceiling — power that serves only itself... stops charging

In Part One we worked on taking back your mind. And here we complete the truth: the version that took back its mind, set its destination, and arrived... stands before a truth written in our very design: **a human being is not completed by eating for himself alone.**

Not for a moral philosophy — for a design reason: the mind you have charges on achievement for a while... and then it deducts. The first success cheque is a high feeling, the third cheque became an ordinary feeling, and the seventh became routine. Its simple Arabic name: **habituation.** And habituation to success is like habituation to any sweet thing: it loses its taste.

And the emptiness that comes after this arrival isn't a sign of a defect — it's an **electronic notification** from inside you: «this version has reached its ceiling... something bigger is waiting.» And that bigger thing isn't more of the same story — it's something of a different kind entirely: an impact in someone else's life.

### Second: Service isn't charity... it's an exchange of a higher level

There's a common understanding of service that turns it into a form of pity: one above gives to one below. This understanding is wrong... and it's wrong with no relation to ethics even — it's an operational error.

Mostafa's service to Madame Zeinab wasn't charity: it was an investment in a currency deeper than money — **meaning.** And that meaning became something between him and himself: you're not spending time... you're spending time on something that stays standing after you get up from the seat.

And in the honesty of this book: there's a real material return, and it reached Mostafa in money and reputation and people — but let's be organized: **the return isn't the reason.** Whoever serves for the return becomes a merchant... and commerce has no meaning in it, it has a calculation. Whoever serves without calculating, finds the return coming to him behind his back — without him opening an account. The simple law of life: whoever plants in people's lives... finds it in his harvest, many times over.

### Third: Your service comes out of your skill... not out of your pocket

The first trap in the door of service: «I don't have money to serve.» And the answer is simpler than the trap: service doesn't want your pocket — it wants **the strongest thing you have** and your time.

Mostafa didn't buy Madame Zeinab a new phone. Mostafa spent what he had: **his skill.** And the equation is fixed in every case: you need to find the intersection point between what you're skilful at... and what people need. That point's name is **your service.** What's required of you is only the question: «What am I good at... and who needs this skilfulness and can't afford to pay for it?»

The answer is always there — because every skill in the world has people who need it and can't manage.

### Fourth: A small sustained service... beats a big one-time service

The last understanding that must be set right before the exercise: impact isn't measured by the size of a single day. It's measured by continuation.

A big campaign on one day... makes one quick laugh and ends. Two hours every week for a year = **104 hours of service.** That's a full share in the life of a neighbourhood. And those 104 hours are built on the same muscles of the whole book: a closed decision (from chapter 2), and the daily watering's stability (from chapter 3), and a return at the smallest degree if a Friday was missed (from chapter 4). Sustained service was never a state of enthusiasm — it's a full planting system.

## Chapter Exercise: Two Hours of Khidmaty

**What you need:** the old destination page, a pen, and one quiet question.

1. **Write your core skill.** Not everything you know how to do — the strongest thing you do: the thing people say about it «this one's an artist at that.» (And remember: a skill doesn't have to be your job. Cooking, teaching, phones, arranging, words that calm.)
2. **Ask: who needs it around me?** In the building, in the street, at your work, online. People who need it **and can't afford to pay for it.**
3. **Set the dose:** two hours a week. Not less... and not more in the first month (service from strength, not from burnout — the self-compassion rule from the storm chapter works here too).
4. **Do the first service this week.** Just one. Small. With your skill — not with your money.
5. **Mark it on the watering page.** A mark of a different kind: the mark that gets counted to the credit of someone else's life. (And for the right arrangement: it's forbidden to do it so you can post it. Posting evaporates the meaning... and this meaning is what you want to harvest.)
6. **The protection rule:** your service has its boundaries. «No» isn't an ugly word in the Khidmaty dictionary — because service without limits turns the sweetest thing in your life into the first thing that burns you.

And four common traps:

- **«I don't have money to serve»:** we talked above — service is from skill and time. Most people have set aside for serving people 10% of the free time that goes to their phone alone.
- **«My service has to be big to count»:** Madame Zeinab's service was one phone. All the rest came after it.
- **«I'll serve when I get myself sorted first»:** The order is flipped — meaning is part of the sorting. A person completes himself while serving... not serving when he's complete.
- **«People will exploit me»:** That genuinely happens when there are no limits — which is why the sixth step in the exercise isn't a luxury.

**The mark of success:** after the first small service with your skill, you'll feel something light in your chest. Not the hero feeling — quieter and deeper: the same «this is mine» feeling you felt on the destination page... but one degree bigger: not «this is mine»... **«this is me.»**

## Sentence of the Week

«**Strong... doesn't mean complete.**»

Write it and put it in front of you. All week, every time you feel your power — in your work, in your system, in your discipline — stop a second and ask: «This power... whom is it serving?» If the answer is still «only me»... then there's something missing in you that doesn't need money or chances: two hours.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

Mostafa is now thirty-four. The workshop became three workshops in three neighbourhoods — and the cardboard with the Friday offer written on it is still there above the counter in all three, in the same hand. And Madame Zeinab has begun telling the story in the salon as an example of «the lovely people in the neighbourhood»... and she never knew she was the one who started the whole story.

The ladder is done. Destination... decision... watering... storms... people... and impact. That's the whole ladder. But before we reach the end and hand you the 90-day plan... there's one last thing in the book: **stories.**

Real people who walked this same ladder — from the first rung to the last. Not celebrities or names in the papers: people like Karim you met in the first chapter, like Tarek and Mona and Dalia and Omar. The next chapter puts these stories next to each other — so you see the ladder walking in the lives of people of flesh and blood... and find your own place among the stories.
