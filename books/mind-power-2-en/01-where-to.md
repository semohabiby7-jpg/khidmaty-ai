# Chapter One: Where To?

There's a question no one asks themselves except in counted moments: the last night of the year, the night of losing someone dear, or a coincidence years in the making. A question of only two words, and if you learn to answer it honestly, you'll save yourself years of running in circles:

"Where to?"

Don't be fooled by how simple it looks. Most people, if asked right now, could tell you everything about their day: the work, the obligations, the problems, the week's plan. But the question isn't "what are you doing?"... it's "where are you going?" — and that's a question about direction, not motion. And the difference between the two is what this whole chapter is about. Get it right, and the next six chapters open up for you without effort... just as the first chapter of Part One opened the rest for you.

And we'll begin the way we always begin... with a story.

## Karim's Story, and the Question at the Station

Karim is thirty-four. An accountant at a firm in downtown. His day is locked by anyone's standards: a seven o'clock wake-up by force, two hours of transport, eight hours at a desk, a one-hour lunch where the same talk repeats at the same table, and at night a phone that takes half his free time. A busy man. And if you asked him "what are you doing with your life?" he'd answer word for word: "I don't have time to breathe."

The story happened on a Thursday night, at a petrol station on the Corniche.

Karim was standing to fill the car, the queue was long, and a guy looked over from the next pump and laughed: "Karim?!"

Khaled. University friend. He hadn't seen him in six years — since graduation day when the class scattered. They hugged in the middle of the station and the small gathering sat on the pavement: "How are you? Where've you been all this time?"

Karim gave the official answer — the one we all give: "Praise be to God. Working. Life goes on. Busy, you know."

Khaled laughed and said it lightly, a joke in a coffee-ground gathering on the ground:

"Busy... going where?"

The question came out without anyone putting weight on it. But what happened after that sentence was strange: Karim opened his mouth to answer... and stopped.

Not because the question was hard. Because the answer wasn't there to begin with. He tried "at my work" — but six years in the same company, same office, same chair. He tried "at my home" — but the home is fixed as it's been since the apartment contract was signed... no, let's say: the home is standing as it is. He tried "at myself" — and laughed at himself from inside, because what he had on that front was exactly what he had on graduation day: the same dreams... postponed the same way.

Khaled didn't feel the pause. He carried on with his stories, left him his number, and they parted on good terms. But Karim caught it that night while washing his face in front of the mirror. He stopped and asked himself the question himself:

"Where am I going?"

And there was no answer.

Let's do Karim's math in numbers — because numbers don't lie:

- Two hours of transport a day, in 250 working days a year = **500 hours** on the road.
- Eight hours at a desk × 5 days × 48 weeks = more than **1,900 hours** at the office.
- Three hours on the phone a day × 365 days = more than **1,000 hours** on the screen.

The total: about **3,400 hours a year**. A full year of a man's life moving in, round the clock, on the hour. The yield of those years in one direction he chose himself, with full clarity: **zero**.

Not because Karim is lazy. Karim is a genuinely tired man — and that's what makes the story hurt. Because he never defined a destination... for a reason that's not written in his ledger.

## The Taxi Meter

There's a scene that's stayed in my head for years: the taxi meter.

The taxi moves more than any car on the street. It circles the whole city every day: east and west, north and south, airport and squares. And the meter beside it keeps counting: five, twenty, a hundred... and by night it's up. The number says "motion." But the number doesn't ask the important question: "going where?" — the meter asks "how much?" and nothing more.

And at the end of the night, the taxi has circled every corner of the city... and not one of them was "its destination." It follows other people's destinations: a passenger wants the courthouse, a passenger wants the school, a passenger wants home. The cars move... and the destination is always someone else's.

And your mind works on exactly that system when you have no destination: it records "how much" — an email answered, a call made, an errand run, an appointment kept — and by the end of the day your feeling is "a full day." But by the end of the year: the same office, the same home, the same postponed dreams. The meter's up... and the place is fixed.

And more dangerous than that: the meter gives a false feeling of progress. "I'm tired" is sometimes read as "I'm moving" — and the two are not the same. The tiredness of motion is real and respectable... but it's not proof of arrival. And that explains something that baffles many people: a man working hard and persistently for years, watching a colleague whose "work is lighter" get promoted and move forward every year. The difference isn't in the motion... it's in the question the meter can't ask.

## The Idea in Points

### First: The mind is a navigation system... and it needs a destination to start

Open Google Maps on your phone. The screen loads fast, the map appears, your location shows in blue, everything's ready. But until you type where you're going... no route will be drawn. The system isn't broken — there's nothing for it to calculate.

And your mind is the greatest navigation system ever born. It calculates routes, compares alternatives, learns from mistakes, and stores every trip you've taken. A magnificent system. But it has one clear condition since it was made: **it needs a destination.** The moment you set one, it works with you at once: it opens a road, draws your attention to something that serves the direction, leaves you a signal on an opportunity along the way.

And if there's no destination? The system doesn't shut down... it switches. It runs "fire-fighting mode": a call — answer. A notification — look. A problem — put it out. A task demanded — done. The system is genuinely running... but you're not the one deciding where the fire is.

Part One taught: "Who doesn't see doesn't choose." That was the first door. And this is the second door, and deeper: **"Who defines no destination... tires out without arriving."** And the big trick is that the tiredness itself gets read as motion — so you keep going for years.

### Second: "Busy" is the most dangerous word in your life

That word has become the official answer to "how are you?". It's said verbatim by everyone to everyone, and few notice that it's not an answer... it's a flight from an answer.

Run this test on the last week of your life, hour by hour:

- The 8 hours of work: "where is this taking me?"
- The three hours on the phone at night: "going where?"
- The gatherings and side jobs: "going where?"

If most of the answers come down to: "so the day passes" — then you're running in neutral: an engine with a loud sound and moving energy... and not a single centimetre moved.

And notice an important distinction before you get sad: being busy itself isn't the disease. Work is honour, and responsibility is nobility. The disease is when "the day passes" becomes the destination. That's why the difference isn't between one salary and another, or one job and another: there are people with modest income walking a clear direction, and people with big income stuck in the same year for years. The meter doesn't ask about the balance... it asks about the direction.

### Third: People's destination isn't yours

The most dangerous thing about destination is that it's inherited.

Your father: "become a doctor or an engineer." The group: "this course is in demand in the market." Your friend: "come, let's start a project." Social media: "everyone travels, be aware." And you follow all of it with real seriousness and effort... and every time you arrive you feel something's missing. Not because you're a failure — because the ladder is solid and sturdy... but it's leaning on a wall that isn't yours. And you wonder why the summit felt hollow: you climbed the ladder... but it wasn't on the wall you wanted.

There's one question that tells a borrowed destination from a real one. Ask yourself about any goal you have:

"If no one would ever know... and no one would ever see... would I still want it?"

If the answer changes the moment you take people out of the equation — then it was someone else's destination, left with you as a trust. And if it stays fixed — then it's yours, and you can spend your life on it with peace of mind.

And here a dangerous carryover from Part One works behind the scenes: "your mind believes what gets repeated." If you think every day about someone else's goal, your mind will be programmed on it and you'll feel it's truly yours. And you'll discover the truth later — on the day you feel your life is missing something while you're standing in the middle of it not understanding why. That's why this check is done now... not after the road.

### Fourth: A destination is a year long... not a whole life

The biggest trap in the "where to?" question: believing you must know the map of your whole life before you move a step. So you choke on the size of the question, so you postpone, so you say "it's still early for this kind of thinking."

No. No one knows the map of their life — not even the author himself. You don't need a life map... you need **a one-year destination.** A year is something you can see: twelve months, fifty-two weeks. And it's set with one question:

"What do I want to be different in my life after 365 days?"

Not "I'll become a millionaire" — decorative dreams turn into pressure. Something you can genuinely picture: a certificate you got and entered a field with. A skill that became a small income source. A body that got in shape. One project that stood up. A mental state different from last year.

And after the destination... no grand plans or Excel files. There's **the step of the day**: a small thing that takes a minute, that you do today, and moves you a centimetre toward it. It's the same One-Minute Rule you learned in Part One — but the second version of its mission: there it was breaking procrastination... here it's gaining distance. Every day you push one in its direction, or you push it into the sack. And that sack is called a life.

## Chapter Exercise: The Destination Page

**What you need:** a sheet of paper, a pen, and one quiet fifteen minutes. And your phone far from you — not off, just far.

1. **At the very top of the page write:** "A year from today... I am:"

2. **Complete the sentence three times only.** Three full sentences — not words: one about your body or health, one about your work or income, one about your state or relationships. If more than three come, don't write any but the three that remain with you after an hour.

3. **Choose one.** If you're torn between two, ask: "If I had only one year... which one would I not let go?" The one you wouldn't let go — that's the destination.

4. **Write the appearance line.** Not the dream itself — its daily effect. Not "I'll be rich"... "I'll wake up without the bill keeping me up in my head." Not "I'll be famous"... "I'll work on something I'm proud to show my daughter when she grows up."

5. **Write the step of the day.** The smallest step, one minute long, that you do before you sleep tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight.

6. **And the page in a fixed place in front of you.** A destination that's written and hidden... gets lost.

And four common traps:

- **"I can't find anything to dream of":** Start from the one thing that bothers you in your life right now. Half the world's destinations were born from the line "I don't want this anymore" — start from it and fold it to the other side.
- **"It has to be big or nothing":** A small one-year destination that got harvested beats a postponed life dream. A big dream burns on its own... a small destination gets harvested alongside others.
- **"I'm not free right now":** Those fifteen minutes are the time of three videos. If there's no quarter of an hour for your year, then the question "where to" has found its real answer.
- **"I'll start at the beginning of the month":** A destination that waits for the first of the month... waits for the new year, and then for a whole life. Those fifteen minutes exist today, exactly.

**The mark of success:** It's not enthusiasm. Enthusiasm is a guest who comes and goes like all guests. The mark of success is something far quieter: as you read the three sentences, one of them will do something strange in your chest. Not a flutter... something like "this one's mine." If you felt it in one of them — then this page is the first page of your life written without inheritance. And hold on to that feeling... you'll need it in the next chapter.

## Sentence of the Week

"Busy... doesn't mean moving."

Write it on your phone and put it in front of you at the desk. All week, every time you hear yourself say "I'm busy" — or hear it from someone — stop for a second and ask: "Busy with what? Going where?" Even if the answer is "I don't know" — the stopping itself is the first answer in the chain. Because who never stops... never defines. And everything that starts from an honest stop... gets a direction.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

Karim didn't get a miracle at the station. He went home, slept, and woke the next day to the same routine. But one small thing changed: the question stayed with him. In transport, at the office, in the face in the mirror. And after three weeks, he wrote the first destination page of his life — something no one would guess: a certificate in financial analysis he'd been saying "I'll do it" to for fourteen years. Not because it's his life's dream... because it was the first thing he found the line "this one's mine" in, without thinking of anyone.

His meter still counts like before. But the difference became clear to him alone: every hour now counts in a direction.

And with that — let me hand you the question that should stay with you for the rest of the week:

Karim needed a coincidence at a petrol station for the question to reach him. You have this chapter — so the question has reached you. But to be honest with you as usual: the question is the easy part of the story. The hard part is what comes after — what separates someone who wrote a destination page and threw it in the drawer... from someone who wrote it and walked behind it.

There's a thing called **a decision.** Not just any decision — the decision that's taken once, costs on its day, and pays its price while you're alone in front of the mirror. There's one night in every life that splits time in two: before it and after it. And the next chapter is about that night exactly — and about the frame that lets a decision stay standing no matter how the storms hit:

**"One decision that changes the direction."**
