# Chapter Seven: Boundaries and Safety — Protecting the System from the World

## The problem

Everything we've built so far can collapse in one second. Your wave starts, you open the file, and a voice comes from the door: "got a minute?" Twenty-five minutes later you stand up not remembering what you were doing.

The system doesn't fail because you're disorganised. It fails because **your boundaries are open** — to the small request, to the message you'll "just answer", to the perfectionism saying "it's not ready", and to your fear of asking for help.

This chapter is about two kinds of boundaries: with **people**, and with **yourself**. And a final part that matters most: **health safety** — when the issue is bigger than a book.

---

## Part One: Boundaries with people

### "Got a minute?"

The small request is more dangerous than the big one. You refuse the big one easily. But "got a minute?" you accept without thinking — and the next hour is gone, because returning to a mental state takes far longer than the minute itself.

**Rule:** not every "minute" costs a minute. It costs 10–20 minutes of recovery.

### The short refusal

Most people fall into explaining and justifying, and long explanations give the other person room to negotiate. A boundary that can be negotiated isn't a boundary.

**Three ready sentences:**
- "Not right now — I'll get back to you at four."
- "I'm mid-something, I'll come back to you when I'm done."
- "Today won't work, tomorrow morning?"

No long apology, no justification, no lying. **A sentence plus a time alternative.** That's all.

### Communication windows instead of "always available"

The problem isn't that people ask — it's that they're used to you being available any time. So any time becomes acceptable.

**Open windows:** "I answer messages at one and eight." And one line to the important people: "If it's urgent, call me, don't message."

After a week something surprising happens: people adjust — not because you became difficult, but because you became **clear**.

### "No" before you start

The first twenty minutes of your day are the most expensive. Start your day answering people and you're starting it on someone else's tasks — then wondering why you can't start your own.

**Rule:** the first twenty minutes are yours. No phone, no messages, no "let me just check what happened".

## Part Two: Boundaries with yourself

### Perfectionism isn't precision — it's procrastination in respectable clothing

Perfectionism says: "it's not ready for people yet, improve it." So you keep improving, it's never ready, and a complete-at-80% thing sits in a drawer for months.

**The 80% rule:** what reaches 80% goes out into the world. The last 20% is learned from the people who use it, not from your head.

### "Finished" doesn't exist — "shipped" does

There's a difference. "Finished" is an open word with no ceiling. "Shipped" has a date and closes the door.

**Swap the word:** not "I must finish this", but "this version ships on such a day". The version isn't final, it's **current**. And a current version beats the imaginary one in your head.

### A fixed time, not open-ended quality

Say "I'll write until it's good" and you've written an open cheque. Time stretches, quality never arrives, blame grows.

**Replace with:** "I'll write for forty-five minutes, then look at what came out." There's a ceiling, a start, and an end.

### Don't promise tomorrow a higher quality

The sentence that ruins everything: "I'll do it better tomorrow." Tomorrow won't be better — tomorrow you'll have the same problem with less energy. If today isn't possible, log twenty poor minutes and move on. Poor work can be fixed; zero doesn't exist.

## Part Three: Boundaries with the screen

### The first and last hour

Two hours decide the day: the first after waking, the last before sleep. If the screen takes them, it has taken the whole day from both ends.

**Rule:** first thirty minutes and last thirty minutes without a screen. Not perfect, not a whole week — just thirty minutes.

### One screenless hour

It doesn't need to be a "detox". One hour a day, fixed, without a screen — not in the middle of your work, but during rest. You'll discover your mind recovers faster than you think.

## Part Four: Health safety

Here I have to be direct, because this is the most important part of the book.

### What this book does not do

This book is about **systems and habits** — not diagnosis or treatment. It is not a substitute for a doctor or a specialist, and it is not a diagnostic tool. If you sense something bigger, the bigger thing gets handled first.

### Signs that call for a professional

Some things belong with a specialist, not a book:
- **Persistent difficulty focusing since childhood**, present in more than one setting (work, home, study) — assessment needs a professional; the decision doesn't come from the internet.
- **Chronic sleep problems**, or heavy snoring with daytime fatigue — needs evaluation.
- **Low mood most of the time**, loss of pleasure, or ongoing social withdrawal.
- **Severe anxiety** or panic attacks.
- **Any thoughts of harming yourself** — this is an emergency, not a book topic. Tell someone now, contact emergency services where you live, or go to the nearest hospital. This book is not the place for it.

### Medical causes that can look like "a focus problem"

Not all fatigue in focusing comes from attention. Several medical issues produce the same symptoms and need a doctor's tests: deficiencies (such as iron), thyroid problems, breathing problems during sleep, depression, anxiety, and medications that affect concentration.

So: **if symptoms are persistent and affecting your life, start with a doctor, not an app.**

### Medication

If a professional prescribed something for you: **don't stop it yourself, don't switch it yourself, and don't start anything yourself.** Any medication decision is a medical decision and has nothing to do with books or systems.

### Co-occurring conditions are not a defect

Many people have more than one thing together — focus and anxiety, or focus and mood. It's common, and it doesn't mean you're "a difficult case". It does mean **full assessment** matters more than a partial fix.

### Emotional safety

What you say to yourself after failure affects the next attempt more than any tool in this book.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "I'm a failure" | "this task needs more breaking down" |
| "I'm lazy" | "this is an energy or clarity problem, not a character problem" |
| "I always fail" | "this time the tool didn't fit" |

Not empty positive thinking — **locating the problem**. A problem in your character has no solution. A problem in the system has one tomorrow.

### One person

You don't need to tell the world. You need **one person** who knows you're working on this, and one simple question a week: "what stopped you?" Accountability to a human is lighter than accountability to yourself.

### Before / after

**Before:** the system works a week and collapses at the first busy week. Blame rises, conclusion: "I'm not the type a system works for."

**After:** clear communication windows, a ready refusal sentence, 80% instead of perfection, and two protected half-hours a day. The system isn't stronger — it's **protected**.

### Chapter rule

> A system without boundaries gets eaten in the first week. Protect what you built, and see a professional when you need one — that's design, not weakness.

### Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Write **three refusal sentences** in your own words.
2. Choose **two windows** for replying to messages and write them down.
3. Write what you'll do in the **first and last thirty minutes** instead of the screen.
4. Draw a line on a sheet: "things I should see a professional about" — list anything you've been carrying for a while.
5. Write the name of **one person** you'll tell that you're working on this.
