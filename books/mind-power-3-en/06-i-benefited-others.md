# Chapter Six: I Benefited Others

You've reached a level you didn't imagine. You determined your destination. You arrived. You evaluated what you have and believed in it. You benefited from it. You led your life with it. Five rungs. And five questions you answered. And every time you answered, you went up a rung. And every time you went up, the next question got deeper.

But the sixth question... this one is different from all that came before. All the previous questions were about you: "Where am I going?" "What did I reach?" "What's its value?" "How do I benefit?" "What do I lead?" All of them — you. You. You.

The sixth question flips the game. It takes you out of yourself... and turns you to people: "Who did I benefit?"

Because everything you did — if it stops at you — it's half a journey. And the other half... the one that completes the meaning... is what you learned reaching someone else. Not to show off. Because that's what makes what you did... have lasting value.

And we'll begin as we always begin... with a story.

## The Story of Karim and the Knowledge That Died with Its Owner

Karim is forty-five. A civil engineer. Worked for twenty years. Built bridges, executed projects, solved technical crises no one else could. On paper — Karim is a walking vault. Every experience he passed = a lesson. Every crisis he solved = wisdom. Every project he completed = accumulated knowledge.

Karim also did what's in this book. Determined his destination (to be a respected engineer). Arrived. Evaluated his work. Believed it was good. Benefited (increased his income, taught assistants, streamlined his work). Led his life (he's the one who makes decisions on his projects).

But there's one thing Karim didn't do. And not because he didn't know how — because he didn't think of it.

Karim has a notebook. The same notebook we talked about in Chapter Four — his vault of experience. Everything he learned in twenty years is written in it. The problem? The notebook is with Karim. And many people who know Karim — know he's smart, know he has experience, know he'd help if asked. But they don't ask. And Karim doesn't give. And the knowledge dies with its owner.

One night, Karim was sitting at the café with his old friend Ahmed. Ahmed was complaining: his son, a fresh engineering graduate, is lost, doesn't know where to start, companies give him the runaround and no one responds. Karim listened. And was moved. And after Ahmed left, Karim stayed alone.

He thought: "I have twenty years of experience. I know the eyes of this profession. I know the mistakes beginners make. I know what companies look for. If I sat with Ahmed's son for an hour... I could change his life. One hour of my life. And after that — he walks."

But he didn't do it. Not because he didn't want to. Because he didn't think it was his role. He thought his role was to be a good engineer. A father to his family. A respected professional. But he didn't think his role — the one who has knowledge — was to deliver it.

And then Karim passed away. God rest his soul. After he passed, Ahmed asked: "Does anyone have Karim's notebook?" No one knew. The notebook was lost. And the knowledge in it — twenty years — went with it. A vault that was full... and was lost.

## The Palm Tree and the One Who Eats from It

There's a tree called the palm tree. One of the best trees. It gives dates. Dates are sweet and filling. And it gives fronds (for shade). And fiber (for rope). And a trunk (for building). One palm tree can serve many people. Each takes something useful from it.

But here's something important about the palm tree: if you don't plant a new palm from it — this palm will grow, live for years, and eventually die. And everything it used to give... stops. The palm that lives only for itself... lasts one generation. The palm that plants others... lasts generations.

And that's the difference between the one who benefits others and the one who doesn't. The one who benefits others — like the palm that plants — their knowledge transfers. The one who doesn't — their knowledge dies with them. And the difference isn't in what they have. Karim had twenty years. The one who helped others had one. But the one who helped — planted a palm. And Karim... didn't plant.

This whole chapter says one thing: what you have... if you don't transfer it... is lost. And if you transfer it... it lives. In someone else.

## The Idea in Points

### First: Knowledge that doesn't transfer... is lost

There's a bitter law: knowledge that doesn't transfer from its owner to someone else... dies. It doesn't transfer. It doesn't get preserved. It's lost. Because knowledge isn't in the notebook — knowledge is in the head. And when the head goes... the knowledge goes. And the notebook without the head that explains it — is paper.

That's what happened with Karim. The notebook existed. But Karim, who could read it — was gone. And the world lost twenty years of experience. Not because he didn't write it. Because he didn't transfer it while alive. Transfer isn't only by writing — transfer is by sitting, explaining, working alongside someone, answering a question. Living knowledge transfers through living. Not through paper.

### Second: Benefiting others isn't charity... it's completing the journey

Some people think of benefiting others as charity or a favor. It's not. Benefiting others is the final stage of your journey. Because your journey isn't complete without it. What you learned, what you did, what you reached — all of it is half a journey if it doesn't reach someone else. The other half... is benefiting others. Without it — the journey is incomplete. Not incomplete in people. Incomplete in you.

Karim was a complete engineer. But his journey was incomplete. Because he didn't transfer. And that's not because people needed it — because he needed to complete. The one who has knowledge and walks away... is like someone who ate bread and didn't get full. A stockpile behind. And hunger ahead.

### Third: Benefiting others doesn't have to be big

Some people think benefiting others has to be a big project. A book, a course, a foundation. No. Benefiting starts small. An hour with a friend's son. Explaining something you know to someone who needs it. A message to someone who's lost. Benefiting isn't about size — it's about direction. Every time you know something and give it to someone who needs it — you're planting a palm. And it doesn't have to be a big palm. A small palm grows.

Karim didn't need to create an engineering course. He needed to sit for an hour with Ahmed's son. One hour. And that would have changed the boy's life. And everything the boy would learn and build... from that hour. Benefiting isn't big. Benefiting is simple. But its impact isn't simple.

### Fourth: You benefit... before you're "ready"

Some people wait until they're "an expert" to benefit others. But that waiting is a trap. Because you'll never be "an expert" in the way you imagine. Every level you reach... there's another above it. And if you wait until you arrive... you won't. Benefit with what you have now. Not with what you'll have tomorrow. What you learned yesterday — someone needs it today. Don't wait.

Karim, if he'd sat with Ahmed's son while "still not fully an expert" — he would have helped. He didn't need to be the best engineer in Egypt. He needed to be someone who learned something... and was willing to explain it. Benefiting doesn't require perfection. It requires intention. And perfection comes along the way.

## Chapter Exercise: The "I Benefited Others" Page

**What you need:** Paper and pen, and five minutes.

1. **At the top, write:** "I know something no one else does." Write five things you know — a skill, an insight, an experience, a lesson you learned. Doesn't have to be big. "I know how to handle an angry customer" counts. "I know how to save money each month" counts. Five.

2. **Below each, write:** "Who needs this right now?" Think. Who in your life — a friend, relative, colleague, a neighbor's child — needs to know this? Write their name. Not "people" — a name.

3. **Choose one of the five... and give it.** Not five. One. One only. Sit with that person. Send them a message. Explain this thing to them. An hour. Half an hour. The duration doesn't matter — what matters is that you delivered.

4. **After you give it... write one sentence:** "Today I transferred knowledge. If I die tomorrow — this knowledge lives."

## Weekly Sentence

**"What you learned and didn't transfer... was half a journey. The other half: teach it to someone else."**

Write it and keep it in front of you. All week, whenever you find someone asking or needing help — ask yourself: "Do I know this?" If "yes" — sit and give it. Don't wait. The knowledge in your head needs to come out.

## Before We Close the Chapter...

Six rungs on the ladder: determined, arrived, evaluated and believed, benefited, led, and benefited others. And each rung built on the one before. And the whole ladder... from the first question "Where am I going?" to the last question "Who did I benefit?"... one complete journey. And the next chapters — the stories and the ending — aren't new rungs. They're what makes the whole ladder visible before your eyes. One vision. From first rung to last. From first question to last. From first day to last day. The next chapter — the stories.
