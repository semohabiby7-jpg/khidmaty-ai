# Chapter One: Your Brain Isn't Defective — It Runs on a Different Reward System

## The problem

You're at the desk. The task is clear: a three-page report.

Four minutes later you're on your phone. Not because you forgot — because your brain decided the report doesn't pay enough for the effort. The task **isn't rewarding enough**, so the brain goes looking for anything that gives a quick hit.

And the advice people give ("just push through") loses every time, because you're fighting a chemical system, not a bad habit. The person who loses that fight daily loses something more important than time: **their confidence.**

## The method: understand what your brain is shopping for

The brain prefers tasks that contain one or more of these four:

| Condition | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **A near reward** | The result lands in minutes, not months | A reply, a game, a scroll |
| **A clear first step** | You know exactly what to do in the first 30 seconds | "Open the file, write a title" — not "write the report" |
| **A sense of urgency** | A near deadline that imposes itself | Due in two hours |
| **Sensory pleasure** | Look, sound, movement, colour | A nice pen, music, a clean notebook |

A task with none of these will be postponed by your brain, however important it is. And the idea that changes everything: **you don't need more willpower. You need to add one of these conditions to the task.**

### Redesigning a task in three minutes

Take any task you keep postponing and ask three questions:

1. **What is the smallest possible first step?** (It must take under two minutes.)
2. **What is the first visible result?** (It has to appear before the day ends so the brain can feel it.)
3. **What form makes it pleasant?** (A notebook, colours, a place, music.)

Example: instead of "build the quote for Ahmed", do "open the file and type the client name and date into the header" (two minutes) + "send the client a draft before noon" (near reward) + "work in the notebook I like, blue pen" (sensory pleasure).

## The tool: the task card

Write a four-line card for every big task:

> **Task:** [name]
> **First step (two minutes):** [a concrete action]
> **First reward:** [what becomes visible, and when]
> **Its shape:** [how I'll do it comfortably]

Those four lines turn an "impossible" task into a "not so bad" one. That is the difference your brain needs.

## Chapter rule

> If a task has been postponed more than three days, the problem isn't your willpower — the task needs to be split smaller, or it needs a closer reward.

## Chapter exercise (10 minutes)

1. Pick one task you've postponed for over a week.
2. Fill in the task card for it.
3. Do only the "first step" — two minutes, then stop.
4. Record whether you did it.

The exercise doesn't ask you to finish the task. It asks you to break the first wall — the rest follows.
