# Chapter Eight: The Ending

## The last thing

We've reached the last pages of the book, and I'm not going to close with a big sentence.

I'll ask you one question, and then I'll leave you.

## The question

In the first chapter, you wrote five sentences you say about yourself.

Take them again, and look at them.

And then ask yourself the question the whole book has been trying to bring you to:

**What would have been different if someone had told you ten years ago that these sentences aren't true?**

You're not being asked to feel regret. Regret takes energy and gives no direction. You're being asked for something smaller: to see that the delay wasn't because the world was locked, but because you were holding the door from the inside.

And there's another thing, more important than regret: **these five sentences are still with you.** Maybe they've changed a little, maybe they've put on new clothes. But one of them is still working. And that's the one we want to see.

## The book's map in two minutes

Before you close it, take the whole book in one page. Because an idea read once gets forgotten, and an idea summarised stays.

**I saw the box:** you learned that a belief isn't an opinion — a belief is a ruling. And it operates without asking your permission.

**I took it apart:** you learned that a belief was built from a sentence + repetition + fear. And if it was built, it can be taken apart. Not in one blow — piece by piece.

**I tried the outside:** you learned that truth isn't changed by talking, it's changed by experiment. And one small step is more honest than a hundred new ideas.

**I found the opportunity:** you learned that an opportunity isn't a shiny idea — an opportunity is a request. Someone wants something, and has something to trade for it.

**I expanded:** you learned that expansion is engineering, not money. What you have is bigger than you think, but it needs to be distributed differently.

**I opened for others:** you learned that the thing that doesn't close with a single door. What you've learned becomes more when you teach it.

Six sentences. If you forget the whole book and memorise these, the book has done its job.

## What won't happen

You won't close the book and wake up as a different person.

Nor will next week be completely different. Nor the month after. Real change is slow to the point of being frustrating.

And here there's a fork in the road:

Some people read a book and get excited for two days, then forget it, then go back to the same place, and say: "The book was nice, but I'm not the type who changes."

That last idea is the same box, in new clothes.

And the worst of it is that it looks like wisdom. A sentence like "I'm not the type who changes" looks like self-acceptance, and it's actually a closed decision. A door locked from the inside, with "this is just how I am" written on it.

## And if you go back to the box?

You will. You have to. You can't come out of an old belief and then walk in a straight line.

One day you'll hear the same old sentence in your head: "You're not up to it." And you'll believe it. And maybe you'll sit for a whole week without doing anything.

The point isn't that you never go back. The point is that when you do go back, you know you've gone back — and that alone is a big difference.

Before this book, going back used to happen without you noticing, so it became "your truth." After it, going back becomes an event: it happens, you see it happening, and then you get up. The one who knows he's fallen can get up. The one who doesn't know stays convinced he's sitting down on purpose.

And keep this in mind: **going back isn't proof the book didn't work.** Going back is proof that you're dealing with something that was built into you over years. The body resists any change, and so does the mind. The resistance itself is a sign that you're moving, not a sign that you've stopped.

## What will happen (if you walk)

If you do just one thing from this book — one small experiment — three things will happen:

**The first:** you'll discover that the fear was bigger than the truth. You were afraid of a reaction, and it turned out to be ordinary. You were afraid of rejection, and rejection turned out easier than you imagined. A small experiment does one important thing: it shrinks fear down to its real size.

**The second:** you'll find yourself asking different questions. Not "why can't I?" but "what do I have, and how do I use it?" And the new question opens a new eye, and the new eye sees things that weren't there in your old sight.

**The third, and the most important:** you'll become the person who opens the door. Not because you've become brave, but because you've come to know it isn't locked. And that knowledge doesn't go away.

## A small thing about "the opportunity that came"

A lot of people will read this book and say: "I can't find an opportunity."

And most of them aren't looking for an opportunity. They're looking for **permission**.

Permission for someone to say: "Go ahead, you deserve it, start." And that permission isn't coming. And most of the time we spend waiting, we call it in our heads "waiting for circumstances," and in reality its name is "waiting for permission."

This book wasn't saying the world is easy. It was saying the door isn't locked, and that the one who looks finds, and the one waiting for permission stays waiting.

## Three things to do after you close the book

I don't want you to close it and say "nice book." I want you to close it holding something.

1. **Take the sheet of sentences** you wrote in the first chapter, and put it somewhere you can see. Not in a drawer. On the wall, or in the phone note you open every day.

2. **Choose one sentence** out of the five. Not all of them. One. And write underneath it: "The small experiment that will break it: ______." And don't do it tomorrow. Do it the day after, and fix the day and the hour.

3. **Choose one person** to tell what you did. Not to boast, but so you say the story out loud. The story that's said out loud settles.

## And if you want to start from a narrow place

If you're in a place that's not helpful at all — work that takes all your time, a home with no space, circumstances that drain you — this book isn't telling you "leave everything and start again." That's the talk of people with a back to lean on.

The book is saying: in that same narrow place, there is at least one unused resource. You don't have to find it today. But start looking for it. The looking itself changes your position from "waiting" to "looking," and the distance between the place of the one waiting and the place of the one looking is a big distance.

## A thirty-day plan after you close the book

Not a big plan. Thirty days, and one thing each week:

**Week one — diagnosis.** Write the five sentences, and choose just one. The one said most, or the one that hurts most. You don't have to know why. What matters is that you choose one and write it on a separate line.

**Week two — the experiment.** Run the seven-day experiment on that sentence. Small, specific, and written down. And every day write one line: what happened. You don't have to write your feelings; write reality.

**Week three — the announcement.** Tell someone. Just one person. Tell him what you're testing, and what happened. Talking turns the experiment from an idea into evidence, and evidence said out loud isn't easily forgotten.

**Week four — the decision.** Make one small decision based on what you saw. Not a life decision. A decision within the scope of a week: one thing you'll do differently from now on. If the experiment told you people accept your work, the decision is "I'll offer it to three people next month." Not "I'll leave my job and start a business."

And if you get to the end of the thirty days and you haven't done anything, don't tear up the paper. Start from week one again. The second attempt isn't weakness — it's the book's own rule: a small step, a review, and repetition.

## Four signs that you're actually changing (even if you can't feel it)

Real change isn't loud. That's why people change for months and don't feel it. Here are four quiet signs you can measure yourself by:

**1) The sentence has become audible.** Before, it operated without your noticing. Now you hear it as you're saying it, and then there's a small pause. That pause is the first sign.

**2) The time you spend in the box has shrunk.** Not disappeared. Shrunk. Before, you'd sit in it two weeks; now, two days. And that's real progress.

**3) You've started asking different questions.** "Why am I like this?" has become "what's the smallest thing I can do right now?" Changing the question is real change, even if the result is still small.

**4) You have evidence now, not just talk.** Something was done, and you can tell it. And the difference between someone telling an idea and someone telling what happened to him is a difference in confidence, not in style.

## Thanks

This book was written by the same rule it preaches: small, specific, and testable.

And if you tried one thing and it worked, make the next one bigger. And if it failed, make the experiment smaller, and don't deny that you tried. The failed experiment that was actually done is more honest than the successful idea that's still on paper.

## Handing over to Part Seven

The next part enters a completely different place: **the words you say to yourself.**

Here we've looked at the sentences that came from outside — from a father, from a teacher, from an experience. But there's another voice, louder than all of those: the voice you tell yourself every day, without noticing. The voice that comments on everything, and translates events before they arrive.

And the question there isn't "how do you silence that voice?" — that's impossible. The question is: **who writes what it says?**

## The sentence to leave with

If this book were summarised in one sentence, it would be this:

**The door is not locked. But nobody will open it in your place.**

Not because you're alone. Because the door opens from the inside, and your hand is the only one there.

I'll see you in Part Seven.
