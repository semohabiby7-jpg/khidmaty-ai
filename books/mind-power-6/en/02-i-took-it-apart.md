# Chapter Two: I Took It Apart

## The story

Ahmed hated mathematics.

Not just hated it. He hated it personally, as if it were someone who had wronged him one day and was still expected to apologise. Every time it came up he'd say: "My brain doesn't take in numbers."

And he was a successful man. He ran a spare-parts shop, priced things in his head, haggled over invoices, and knew the extra pound before the calculator was even open. So the man was genuinely a numbers man. Numbers harder than anything in a schoolbook, calculated on the ground in real life, with people holding him accountable for them.

I asked him once: "I understand you say your brain doesn't take in numbers, but you do this every day, and you do it better than other people."

He was quiet for a moment, wiped the glass of tea in front of him without drinking from it, and said: "That's different. That's in the shop."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. It's just — when I see a sheet with a problem on it, I feel like I'm about to be embarrassed."

I said: embarrassed in front of whom? You're alone at the desk.

He said: "I don't know. I feel like someone's watching me."

Notice that last sentence: **"I feel like someone's watching me."** A man of forty-five, sitting alone in his office, with a whole classroom standing in his head, staring at him.

There it was. Not mathematics. **The sheet of paper.** And the seating chart. And the teacher's hand raising the chalk to the board, waiting. And the sound of the class laughing when someone got it wrong. And the feeling that never passes, the feeling still sitting in his body twenty-five years later.

The sentence had never been "I don't understand numbers." The sentence was "I was humiliated in front of people once, and I'm not going through that again."

And Ahmed didn't hate mathematics. Ahmed hated what happened to him inside that classroom.

And after he saw the difference, something small happened: he caught himself saying "mathematics" when he meant "humiliation," for the first time. From that moment, there was something he could work on. Because you can't work on "humiliation" — but you can work on "a situation."

## What built the sentence?

No belief is born in one moment. It's built in four stages, and if you remove one of them, the belief loosens.

### Stage one: the sentence

One sentence, at a sensitive moment.

It doesn't have to be cruel. It doesn't have to come from someone important. It could be a passing line from a teacher you don't even remember, a laugh, or "why don't you ever say anything, you're always quiet," or a relative saying it as a compliment: "You're quiet like your father, you can't express yourself."

What marks the sentence isn't its cruelty — it's that **it arrived at a moment when you had no defence.** The proof: you remember the scene in detail, you remember who was standing where — and you don't remember what you were wearing.

The sentences that turn into beliefs arrive in a moment of **pressure**, not in a moment of discussion. And every word said in a moment of pressure gets sealed with red wax.

And there's a note here that relieves a lot of people: **not every belief came from a sentence.** Some beliefs are built without a single word, out of a scene: a father worn out saying "work isn't worth it," a mother going silent in an argument, a house where everyone stays up late at each other's expense. The child takes the whole scene and extracts a law from it. And that law operates exactly like a sentence, except you'll never find a written text for it.

That's why in the exercise at the end of this chapter, we don't only ask "who said it to you?" We also ask "what's the first situation you remember?"

### Stage two: repetition

Repetition doesn't require someone saying it to you. The repetition that kills you quietly is the one you say to yourself.

After the first time, you start hearing it in your head before any similar situation. Before you even try, it gets said. And that's the most dangerous repetition: **the sentence you say to yourself becomes your voice.** And nobody argues with their own voice.

And there's a third kind of repetition, the nastiest: repetition through **behaviour**. You stay silent every time, and the silence itself says to you: "See, you're silent, so that's who you are." The act becomes evidence, running in a circle with itself.

That circle is what makes a belief feel like a fact rather than an opinion. You see yourself silent, so you conclude you're "not a talker," and then you stay silent more, to stay consistent with the conclusion. And consistency is something the brain loves, because contradiction hurts.

### Stage three: fear

Here the matter closes.

The first time you went against the sentence and something bad happened — real or imagined — fear fixes the sentence in place. And the sentence offers a clear service: **it keeps you from the pain of repeating it.**

That's why a belief is hard to dismantle: you're not defending an opinion, you're defending **protection**.

And the fear here usually isn't fear of the big thing. Not fear of failure as a word. Fear of a small moment: the second of embarrassment, the world's face looking at you while you don't know what to do, the silence after your mistake.

And there's another fear, less talked about: **the fear of betrayal**. If you've lived your whole life as "the quiet man who never asks for anything," and suddenly you start asking, something in you will feel like you've betrayed someone old. Probably you've betrayed the image of someone you love, or the image of yourself that lived for years. And the fear of that betrayal stops more people than the fear of rejection does.

### Stage four: the payoff

Yes — there's a payoff. And this will annoy people, but it's true.

The sentence gave you something. It protected you from embarrassment, from extra work, from a responsibility you could have been held accountable for, from a disappointment you were afraid of. And anything that gives you a payoff will resist being dropped — because dropping it means losing the payoff, with no guaranteed replacement.

Here you have to be honest with yourself and not soften the words. If your sentence is "I'm not a responsibility person," the payoff is that nobody loads you with anything, nobody holds you accountable, and if something goes wrong, you're not the one who falls. If your sentence is "I'm not a money person," the payoff is that you don't have to calculate, you don't have to say no to yourself, and you don't have to face that there are things you want and don't know how to reach.

That payoff isn't a flaw in you. It's intelligence. The self protects itself with whatever is available — and if what's available is a sentence, it will use the sentence.

Which is why a sentence isn't dropped by being convinced. It's dropped when you build a replacement that delivers a similar or better payoff. Not "convince yourself you deserve it." No. "Do something that gives you the same safety, more cleanly."

## How does the sentence become law?

No official body certifies a belief. Something else happens, and it's simple and frightening: **you go looking for evidence to prove it.**

This is called confirmation bias, and it isn't a moral defect — it's how the brain operates: it selects from the world whatever agrees with what it's already sure of. And the brain doesn't do it to annoy you; it does it to save energy. If every piece of information had to be examined from scratch, nobody would survive a day.

If you're certain you're "not a talker," you'll pass twenty situations where you spoke well and you won't see them. And you'll remember the one time you fumbled badly, and keep it. And sit there saying: "See? I'm aware of myself."

If you're certain "opportunities don't exist," you'll read the same post a thousand people read, and you won't see the opportunity inside it. And you'll say: "It's all talk."

And it doesn't stop at what you see. It reaches what you remember. The brain stores the scenes consistent with the story, and throws away the rest, or distorts them. That's why your memories about yourself aren't a neutral tape — they're **an edited story**, and the editor has an opinion.

And there's a third thing, more dangerous than both: **you manufacture the evidence yourself.** If you're certain people don't like you, you'll walk into any room with your face closed, you won't smile, you'll answer drily. And then you'll say: "See? Nobody talked to me." And you're the one who did that. A belief doesn't just read reality — it shapes it.

## Three levels of a belief

To know what exactly you're dismantling, you need to separate three things:

**Level one: the idea.** "Numbers are hard." A surface idea, changed by a piece of information, said in ordinary conversation.

**Level two: the rule.** "I'm not a numbers person." This has become a rule about yourself, not about numbers. The rule operates across many situations, and blocks the experiment before it starts.

**Level three: the identity.** "I'm the one who doesn't understand." Here it's a story about being. And any evidence against it doesn't break it — it gets attacked. Because disagreement isn't wrong information, it's a threat to what you know about yourself.

And dismantling starts at level one and arrives at level three. The common mistake is that people try to start at level three: "I'm confident, I'm strong, I deserve it." That's a collision attempt with no weapon. It won't work, because identity doesn't change by talk; it changes by an accumulation of small evidence at level two.

That's the order in this book: **see the sentence (chapter 1), break it into rules (chapter 2), create evidence (chapter 3).** And evidence is what touches identity — not the motivational line.

## Four steps to take it apart

### Step one: write it down, word for word

Not "I have a confidence problem." Write the sentence that gets said in your head before the situation. Word for word, in the language it's said in, even if it's ugly slang.

For example: "If I speak, I'll look stupid." Or "If I ask, they'll say I'm greedy." Or "I'm not one of those people."

If you can't find the sentence, review the last three times you withdrew. What did you say to yourself in the moment right before you withdrew? That's it.

### Step two: ask it four questions

1. **What's the evidence?** Not "what are the reasons" — what specifically, and when did it happen.

2. **What is it preventing?** What good thing is this sentence protecting?

3. **Who benefited from it?** Whose interests did this sentence serve at some point? Sometimes you find it served someone else entirely — your father, your teacher — not you.

4. **If it were wrong, what's the first thing that would change?** This question exposes how much you've invested in it.

### Step three: separate the sentence from the incident

The sentence: "I'm a failure at speaking."
The incident: "In a meeting on 4 March, I fumbled and said something incoherent, and someone laughed."

The incident is limited: a day, a situation, some people, an outcome. The sentence is open over an entire lifetime. Put them side by side and you see the sentence is many times larger than the incident. That's not optimism, that's arithmetic.

### Step four: write a temporary version

Not "I'm strong at speaking." That's a lie, and the brain rejects lies quickly.

Write: "Not sure. I tried once and such-and-such happened. Haven't tried it in this situation yet." This temporary sentence is the bridge. It doesn't ask you to believe something big; it asks you to suspend the verdict until you have evidence.

## What happens after you take it apart?

You need to know this so you're not surprised.

The moment you dismantle the sentence, you won't feel freedom. You'll feel a void, and sometimes more anxiety. Because the sentence was protecting you, and once you remove it, there's an open situation that needs a decision — and that decision is responsibility.

Many people return to the sentence at exactly this moment — not because it's right, but because it's comfortable. If that happens to you, know that you're in the right place, and don't go back. That void is the space you'll work in during the next chapter.

## Objections

**"So I just tell myself nice things?"** No. We're not adding nice words. We're removing inaccurate words. The difference is large: the first asks for belief, the second asks for accuracy.

**"But I really am not good at this thing."** Maybe. Nobody said you were. The question isn't "good or not." The question is: "Is this sentence about **the thing**, or about **your worth**?" The difference between "I don't know how to drive" and "I'm a failure" is the difference between describing a skill and passing judgment on a human being.

**"And if the sentence is true?"** Then you've learned something useful: you have a missing skill, and skills can be learned. The damaging sentence isn't the one that says "you don't know how." The damaging sentence is the one that says "and you never will, and you have no right to try."

## Exercise: dissecting the belief

Take one page and divide it into four boxes:

**Box one:** the sentence, word for word, and the first situation you remember.

**Box two:** the evidence you cite for it — write it with dates, as it is.

**Box three:** the payoff the sentence gives you in your life right now.

**Box four:** a temporary version, containing doubt, and limited to a situation.

When you're done, ask yourself one last question and write the answer: **"If someone said this sentence about himself to me, what would I tell him?"**

Your answer is the thing you needed to hear for years. And it may be the first time you say it to yourself without feeling like you're indulging yourself.

## Four mistakes in dismantling that make the belief stronger

Dismantling isn't an attempt to convince yourself of something new. It's precise work, and there are mistakes that make the belief settle deeper:

**1) Arguing with the sentence.** The moment you tell yourself "that's wrong," the belief defends itself and brings evidence. Better: instead of arguing, ask "when did I learn this? and who taught me?"

**2) Dismantling with words alone.** Tell yourself "I'm capable" a hundred times and feel unconvinced. Of course — words need evidence. The belief was built by experience; it comes apart by experience.

**3) Dismantling everything at once.** "I'm a failure at everything" → so you try to convince yourself "I'm successful at everything." That isn't dismantling, that's an inverted inflation of the old belief. The right way: dismantle one small piece that can be tested.

**4) Dismantling by indicting whoever taught you the sentence.** If you turn the dismantling into a fight with your father or your teacher, you'll leave the belief exactly where it is in order to preserve the relationship. Dismantling isn't putting anyone on trial. It's freeing yourself.

And the rule that sums all of this up: **don't try to convince yourself of a new truth. Try a new truth for seven days, and let the result do the convincing.** That's the next chapter.

## And if the sentence came from someone you love?

The hardest belief is the one that came from someone you love. The father who said "we're not talking people" was protecting you from disappointment — he wasn't locking you in.

An inherited belief arrives with a duty of loyalty. And if you dismantle it, you feel like you're betraying someone.

The distinction: **you're not calling the person a liar — you're just not walking on their sentence.** Honour your father, and don't take his decision. That isn't betrayal. That's completion.

## The sentence of the week

**A belief isn't a verdict on the world... a belief is a verdict on yourself, written in the language of the world.**

## Next chapter

The sentence is on paper now. You know its shape, you know what it protects, and you know it builds its own evidence.

So — what breaks it?

Not talk. Not conviction. Not prayer alone.

One thing breaks it, and it's the cheapest thing in the world and the most dangerous thing to a belief: **a small, real experiment, outside your head.**
